A dictation app turns speech into text at your cursor. ChatGPT is a general chat assistant in its own window, and an AI work assistant works on the task already open, then returns a finished draft, rewrite, or summary.\n\nThose three names get mixed up all the time. Some products blend two of the jobs, and a few overlap all three. The jobs are still different. Picking the wrong one costs you a few minutes each time, and those minutes add up over a workweek. This guide shows what each tool takes in, what it gives back, what it can see, and where it stops.\n\n## What each tool takes in and gives back\n\nStart with the input and the output. Those two things explain most of the difference.\n\nA dictation app takes your voice and gives you text. It writes the words you say. It does not decide whether those words make a good reply. Say “I can do Thursday but not before noon,” and you get that sentence with punctuation added. Nothing more.\n\nChatGPT takes a prompt and gives you a reply. You can type the prompt or speak it. The reply lands in a chat window, so you copy the parts you need into your email, document, or code editor. It is strong on open questions, such as comparing two plans or brainstorming names for a project.\n\nAn AI work assistant takes a goal and gives you a result. The result might be a reply, a rewrite, a summary, or a short list of next steps. The better tools read the text or screen around your goal, so you do not paste it in by hand. That is the main thing that sets this category apart.\n\nThe category is not one product. Apple’s Writing Tools and Microsoft Copilot both count as assistants, but each one sits inside one ecosystem. Writing Tools work in most places you write on a Mac. Copilot works inside Microsoft apps, and what it can see depends on your license. The sections below explain both limits.\n\n## AI work assistant vs dictation app vs ChatGPT at a glance\n\n| | Dictation app | ChatGPT | AI work assistant |\n| --- | --- | --- | --- |\n| Input | Your voice | A typed or spoken prompt, plus anything you paste | Your goal, your voice or typing, and the context around it |\n| Output | Raw text at the cursor | A reply in a separate chat | A draft, rewrite, summary, or next step in your current work |\n| Context it uses | Only the words you say | Only what you paste or upload | Depends on the product, your plan, and your permissions |\n| Best at | Exact wording, notes, long messages | Research, brainstorming, open questions | Finishing a task in the app you already use |\n| Main limit | Does not judge tone, facts, or the thread | You carry context and results back by hand | Needs a clear goal and a human check |\n\nUse the table as a quick filter. If you already know the words, use dictation. If you need ideas, use ChatGPT. If the answer depends on something already on your screen, use a work assistant.\n\n## How dictation handles words you already have\n\nDictation is the fastest way to get your own words into a document. Apple’s built-in Dictation starts from the Microphone key, the Dictation shortcut, or Edit > Start Dictation. On a Mac with Apple silicon, you can keep typing while you speak. Apple says there is no need to stop dictation before you type.\n\nTwo limits matter in daily use. First, Dictation stops on its own when it hears no speech for 30 seconds. If you pause to think in the middle of a paragraph, you have to start it again. Second, Apple says Dictation has no time limit on text length. A long memo works fine. The pause is the limit that gets in your way.\n\nApple also says that in macOS 27, Dictation uses an on-device model to improve spelling, punctuation, and capitalization. That feature works in English on Mac models with M3 or later and at least 12GB of unified memory. If your Mac is older, or you dictate in another language, you will not get it. Check your chip and memory under Apple menu > About This Mac before you count on it.\n\nSpeed is the reason many people switch. A 2016 study by Ruan and colleagues tested English text entry on touchscreen phones. Speech reached 153 words per minute, against 52 words per minute on the keyboard. The authors ran it in a lab under what they called ideal conditions, with an iPhone 6 Plus and a Baidu speech recognizer. The corrected error rate was 5.30% for speech and 11.22% for the keyboard. Errors left in the final text were higher for speech, at 1.30% against 0.79%. Speed is real, but checking still matters.\n\nDictation also has a blind spot. It does not read the thread. If the email you are answering asked three questions, dictation writes the answer you say. It will not remind you about the third question. It will not fix the tone either. You have to say what you mean and check what came out. For a side-by-side look at the built-in options, read [Apple dictation vs AI dictation on Mac in 2026](/blog/apple-dictation-vs-ai-dictation).\n\n## When ChatGPT is the right tool\n\nChatGPT works best when the work starts blank. Examples include “Compare three project tools for a five-person team” and “Explain how a retry queue works, in plain language.” Nothing on your screen is needed for those. A good chat gives you options and reasoning, and you can push back on both.\n\nIts voice features come in two modes. OpenAI’s help page draws the line: “Use voice dictation when you only want to turn speech into prompt text before sending it,” and “Use ChatGPT Voice for a live conversation with ChatGPT.” Dictation gives you prompt text you can edit before you send. Voice is a spoken back-and-forth.\n\nAccess depends on your plan. According to OpenAI, ChatGPT Voice in the desktop app is available with Plus, Pro, Business, Edu, and Enterprise plans. Only one voice chat can be active across the desktop app at a time. Availability also depends on rollout status and workspace settings, so check the [OpenAI Learn voice page](https://learn.chatgpt.com/docs/features/voice) before you plan around it.\n\nThe main tradeoff is the handoff. Say you are answering a client email. You copy the thread, paste it into the chat, explain the situation, get a draft, and paste the draft back. Each step is small. Over a full workday, they add up. ChatGPT is still a good second opinion on a tough reply. It is a poor default for every email in your inbox. For a direct comparison, see [Lazur vs ChatGPT for Mac](/compare/lazur-vs-chatgpt).\n\n## What an AI work assistant adds to a task\n\nA work assistant earns its place in the last mile of a task. Four requests show the pattern:\n\n- Reply to this thread and keep it under five sentences.\n- Make this paragraph friendlier, but keep the price the same.\n- Summarize this thread and list the open decisions.\n- Explain what this error message means for the file I have open.\n\nEach request depends on something you can already see. In a chat tool, you copy that material first. An assistant that reads the open text can answer in place. That difference saves the most time on work you repeat every day.\n\nApple’s Writing Tools show what this looks like on a Mac. Proofread checks grammar and spelling. Rewrite offers Friendly, Professional, or Concise tones, or a change you describe. Summarize offers Summarize or Key Points. Apple says Writing Tools are available in most places you write, including third-party apps and websites. Apple also says Apple Intelligence on macOS 27 is not available in all languages or regions, and usage limits may apply.\n\nMicrosoft’s approach depends on your license. Microsoft says Copilot Chat (Basic) and Microsoft 365 Copilot (Basic) responses are grounded in public web data. They cannot use organizational data through Microsoft Graph. You must upload content with the prompt, or use content already open in Outlook or Teams. Microsoft 365 Copilot (Premium) uses web and organizational data automatically. Copilot in Word, Excel, PowerPoint, and OneNote at standard access needs no add-on.\n\nSo ask one question before you pick any work assistant: what can it see? If the answer is “only what I paste,” it behaves like a chat tool.\n\n## Context: what each tool can see\n\nContext decides how much copying you do. Dictation sees the words you speak. ChatGPT sees what you type, paste, or upload. An AI work assistant may see the text or screen in front of you, depending on the product and the permissions you grant.\n\nMore context saves steps, and it also means more to trust. Some Mac AI tools ask for Accessibility or Screen Recording permission, and each one covers different things. If you want the detail, read [the two permissions your AI assistant asks for](/blog/accessibility-vs-screen-recording-ai-permissions).\n\nCheck each vendor’s data policy before you paste client material. This guide does not verify how every product stores audio or text, so read the privacy page for each tool you use.\n\n## Output: raw text, a reply, or a finished draft\n\nThe output decides how much editing you do.\n\nDictation gives you raw text. Expect to fix punctuation, names, and numbers. You will fix more if you speak fast, use jargon, or dictate in a noisy room.\n\nChatGPT gives you a clean reply in its own window. You copy it into your app and adjust it there. That step is quick, but it is still a step, and the adjustments often repeat the same changes.\n\nAn AI work assistant gives you a draft where you are working. If it reads the thread, the draft uses the right names and answers the right questions. You still review it. A draft that reads well can still have the wrong date. Read it once before you send.\n\n## What each option costs and what you need\n\nPrices change often, so check the vendor page on the day you buy. A dictation app and a work assistant remove different steps. Compare them on the steps they remove from your day, not on the sticker price alone.\n\n## Three jobs and the right pick for each\n\n**A client reply with three questions and a deadline.** Dictation can capture your answer, but it will not remember the questions. ChatGPT can draft a reply, but you have to paste the thread first. A work assistant that reads the open thread can answer all three questions in place. If you already know the answers, dictate them and tidy the draft in your app.\n\n**A launch plan from a blank page.** Use ChatGPT. Ask for a first plan, add what you know, then challenge its assumptions. If you think faster out loud, dictate your ideas, then paste the transcript into the chat as the starting point.\n\n**A long memo built from scattered notes.** Start with dictation to get the words out, since dictation has no time limit on text length. Then use an assistant to summarize the draft into key points and proofread it. Apple’s Summarize and Key Points options do this on a Mac. The two tools cover different steps, so use them in sequence.\n\n## Mistakes people make when they choose\n\n1. Buying a work assistant for dictation alone. Built-in Dictation is already on your Mac, so start there and see where it falls short.\n2. Using ChatGPT for every reply. The copy and paste loop slows down routine email.\n3. Trusting the first transcript. Names, numbers, and dates are where dictation errors cost you. The Ruan study found more errors left in final text for speech than for the keyboard.\n4. Forgetting the 30-second pause. A long thought can end the session before you finish the sentence.\n5. Assuming a feature works in your language or region. Apple says Apple Intelligence on macOS 27 is not available in all languages or regions.\n6. Ignoring the plan. Microsoft’s grounding depends on your license, and ChatGPT Voice depends on your plan.\n\n## How to test which one fits your week\n\nMost people have not settled on a routine yet. A Gallup survey fielded from January 26 to March 24, 2026, found that 28% of US employees use AI at least weekly. Another 54% said they had not used AI in their role. A small trial is a sensible way to choose.\n\nPick three tasks you do every day. Run each one through the tool you think fits. Keep a simple tally for a week. Count three things: how many times you copied text between windows, how many times you fixed a transcript or draft, and how many minutes it took until the result was ready to send. The tool with the fewest copies and fixes wins that task.\n\nKeep the tally honest. The first day always feels slow because you are learning the tool. Judge the second and third days.\n\n## Where Lazur fits\n\nLazur is a voice assistant for the Mac that lives in the notch. Hold ⌥ ⌘ to ask about anything on screen, and Lazur answers out loud.
