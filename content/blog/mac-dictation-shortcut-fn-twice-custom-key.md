Your Mac dictation shortcut lives in Apple menu > System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation. Pick Press Fn (Function) Key Twice, the Microphone key on keyboards that have one, or Customize to press any combo you like, such as Option-Z.

That sounds like more choice than anyone needs. In practice you pick one, test it in a text field, and move on. This guide covers each option, how to set it up, the apps that steal the key, and the limits Apple documents, so you know what to expect before you start talking.

## How to set the Mac dictation shortcut

1. Open the Apple menu, choose System Settings, and open Keyboard. Find the Dictation section.
2. Turn Dictation on. If macOS asks, click Enable.
3. Open the Shortcut pop-up menu. Choose Press Fn (Function) Key Twice, or choose Customize.
4. If you chose Customize, press the keys you want. Apple's example is Option-Z.
5. Test it. Open Notes, click into a blank note, press your shortcut, say one sentence, then press Escape.

Apple's Mac help page covers macOS 27 and describes these same steps. Labels can shift between releases. If your wording differs, stay in the Dictation section and look for the Shortcut pop-up. For a longer walkthrough, read [how to use dictation on Mac for macOS 27](/for/developers/problems/mac-dictation-start-stop-change-shortcut).

## When the shortcut is missing or greyed out

Sometimes the setting is missing, or you cannot change it. Start with the basics. Open Keyboard in System Settings and confirm Dictation is switched on. If it is on but nothing happens, check that the shortcut you picked exists on your keyboard. A keyboard without a Microphone key will not start Dictation with that key, for example.

If the Dictation controls are greyed out, your Mac may be managed. Work and school Macs can have settings locked by an administrator. In that case, ask the person who manages the Mac rather than chasing a workaround.

One more cause catches people out. Keyboard layouts and input sources can change which keys you have. After you switch layouts, open the Dictation pane again and confirm the shortcut is still set.

## Which shortcut should you pick?

Start with your keyboard, then with your habits.

If your keyboard has a Microphone key in the function row, that key is the simplest option. You press it once to start and once to stop. There is nothing new to remember.

If your keyboard has an Fn or Globe key but no microphone key, double-pressing Fn is the next simplest choice. It is the setting Voibe's guide calls the default. You can use it on a laptop without learning a new habit.

If you use an external keyboard without either key, or Fn already does a job for another tool, pick Customize. A combo you choose yourself can match the same keys on every keyboard you own. That consistency matters more than people expect once you switch between a laptop and a desk setup.

## Choosing a shortcut for two Macs or two keyboards

If you use a laptop at home and a desktop at work, one setting may not suit both. Keyboards differ. A laptop with an Fn key and an external keyboard with a Microphone key need different choices, so you can end up remembering two shortcuts.

The simple rule is to pick one shortcut per machine, write it down, and test it once on each. Customize is the better choice when you want one habit everywhere. Choose a combo that exists on both keyboards, such as Option plus a letter, then test it on both before you rely on it.

Remappers make this harder. A remapper on one Mac can break the setting there while the other Mac works fine. That is one more reason to test on each machine, not just the one you set up first.

## The three options compared

| Setting | What it needs | How you start and stop | Main trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Press Fn (Function) Key Twice | An Fn or Globe key | Double-press to start, then Escape or the shortcut to stop | Key remappers can block it |
| Microphone key | A mic key in the function row | Press and release to start, press and hold for Siri | A long press opens Siri instead |
| Customize | Any keyboard | Your combo starts and stops | You must avoid combos other apps use |

## Option 1: Press Fn twice

Fn twice is the option most people mean when they search for a Mac dictation shortcut. When you choose it, the Keyboard setting reads Start Dictation (Press Fn Twice). Apple's page uses two names for the key, fn and Globe, so do not worry if your keyboard says Globe.

Voibe's guide, from a company that sells a dictation app, calls Fn or Globe pressed twice the default. Your own Shortcut pop-up is the final word, because the default can differ by keyboard and macOS version.

Worked example: open Notes and click into a new note. Double-press Fn, then say *Meeting moved to Thursday, send the agenda by Wednesday.* Press Escape. The text appears where your cursor sat.

If nothing happens, the two presses may be too far apart, or another app grabbed the key. The conflicts section below covers the usual suspects.

## Option 2: The Microphone key

Apple says the Microphone key appears only on some keyboards, in the row of function keys. Press and release it to start Dictation. Press and hold it to activate Siri, which has to be turned on first. You stop Dictation with the Microphone key again, with Escape, or with your shortcut.

Voibe's guide says the key is F5 on 2021 and newer MacBooks, and that one press starts dictation. Apple's page does not say that, so look at the top row of your own keyboard for a microphone icon.

Worked example: open Mail and start a reply. Tap the Microphone key, say your sentence, then press Escape. A long press is an easy way to end up in Siri, so keep your taps short.

## Option 3: Your own key combo

Choose Customize, then press the keys you want. Apple's example is Option-Z. Voibe's guide also lists Left, Right, or Either Command, Control, or Option pressed twice as choices. Apple's page does not list those, so check your own pop-up before you plan around them.

Pick a combo that does not type a character you need. Then test it in a text field, so a clash shows up before you are halfway through an email.

Also avoid any combo you already use in another app. Check that app's keyboard shortcut list, since many apps use Option and Command combos for their own menus.

## How long dictation runs before it stops

Apple says Dictation stops when you press Escape, when you press the Microphone key if you have one, or when you press your shortcut. It also stops by itself after 30 seconds without detected speech. Apple's page says you can dictate text of any length without a timeout.

So length is not your limit. Silence is. A pause to think for longer than 30 seconds ends the session. Press your shortcut again to start a new one, then keep going.

Worked example: you dictate a long draft in one sitting. Talk through it, pausing for breath or a short think. The session holds until you stop talking for 30 seconds or press Escape.

## What a session looks like from start to finish

Start with the cursor in a text field. A blank note in Notes is a good first test. Press your shortcut, then speak in full sentences at a normal pace, the way you would say it to a colleague. Pause between thoughts, and keep each pause under 30 seconds. When you finish, press Escape, then read the text once.

Here is a realistic run. A colleague asks whether Thursday works. You press Fn twice, say two short sentences about Thursday and the agenda, and press Escape. The speaking is the quick part. The reading takes longer, and it is where you catch a wrong name or a missing word. Spend your effort there.

## Apple Intelligence and on-device punctuation

Apple's page says that with Apple Intelligence, dictation uses an on-device model to handle spelling, punctuation, and capitalization. That feature is available in English on M3 or later Macs with at least 12GB of unified memory.

If your Mac is older, or you dictate in another language, you will not get that model. Open the Dictation section and read what your Mac offers. If punctuation annoys you, this is the setting to check first.

## When another app steals the shortcut

A common cause of a dead shortcut is another tool, not Dictation itself. Voibe's guide lists Karabiner-Elements as the most common conflict. A remap of Fn or F5 can block the built-in double-press. It also names BetterTouchTool, Raycast, Hyperkey, and Rectangle Pro as tools that can clash. Voibe sells a dictation app, so treat that list as a starting point, not a verdict.

Worked example: you remap Fn in Karabiner-Elements for some other job. The Mac never sees the double press, so Notes stays silent. The fix is to remove the Fn rule, or to switch to Customize and choose a combo your remapper ignores.

Work through this order. Confirm Dictation is on. Test in Notes. Then check each remapper's key rules. Change one thing at a time, so you know which change fixed it.

## How much faster is speaking than typing?

Stanford researchers compared speech and keyboard entry for short messages on touchscreen phones, in English and Mandarin. For English, speech ran at 153 words per minute against 52 on the keyboard, a 2.93x speedup. Corrected errors during entry were 5.30% for speech and 11.22% for the keyboard.

The finished text tells a different part of the story. Uncorrected errors in the final message were 1.30% for speech and 0.79% for the keyboard. Speech produced fewer corrected errors while you wrote, but it left slightly more mistakes in the final text. You still need a read-through.

This study does not test Mac dictation, long documents, or your own voice. Treat the numbers as a rough guide. To check your own rate, try the [speaking vs typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test) or the [typing speed test](/tools/typing-speed-test).

## Mistakes people make

- Choosing a Customize combo that another app already uses. The shortcut may then fail in some apps and work in others.
- Holding the Microphone key too long, which opens Siri instead of Dictation.
- Assuming the setting follows you to another keyboard. Check the Dictation section after you switch keyboards.
- Judging dictation after one try. Test three or four short sentences in Notes before you decide.
- Trusting the first draft. Read dictated text once with fresh eyes, especially names, dates, and numbers.
