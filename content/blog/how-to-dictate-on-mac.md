To dictate on a Mac, open **Apple menu > System Settings > Keyboard**, select **Dictation**, and turn it on. Then click into the text field where the words should go, press your dictation shortcut, speak, and stop with **Escape**, the **Microphone key**, or the shortcut again.

This guide walks through the whole setup. You will turn Dictation on, choose the right microphone, set a shortcut you can press, and fix the cases where nothing appears. Apple's current Dictation help page covers macOS 27, so the menu names below follow that version. If your Mac runs an older release, a few labels may differ.

## What voice to text on a Mac actually means

Voice to text on a Mac is live dictation. Words appear at the cursor while you speak. You do not upload a recording, wait for a file to convert, or paste from a separate window.

People search for the same job under several names:

- **Mac dictation** or **Apple dictation**: the built-in feature in macOS.
- **Voice to text Mac**: often from people coming over from Windows or Google Docs.
- **Mac voice recognition**: often means the feature is on, but the Mac is not hearing you. The microphone section below covers that.

Dictation is for writing. Apple's page says general text Dictation is for composing messages and notes, not for dictating in a search box. If you want to say "open Safari" or "click Save", you need Voice Control. That is a separate feature with its own settings.

## Turn on Dictation in System Settings

You only do this part once.

1. Click the **Apple menu**, then choose **System Settings**.
2. Click **Keyboard** in the sidebar.
3. Find **Dictation** and turn it on. If macOS asks, click **Enable**.
4. Confirm the language under Dictation. Apple does not offer dictation in every language or region. If yours is missing, check Apple's macOS feature availability page before you blame your microphone.

The same Keyboard settings also show whether general dictation is processed on your Mac or sent to Siri servers. Read that line if privacy matters to your work. Apple's current page does not say plainly whether dictation works offline, so that line is your best check.

## Pick the microphone Dictation uses

Under Dictation, find the **Microphone source** pop-up menu. **Automatic** picks the device you are most likely to dictate into. That works on most days. It fails when you switch between AirPods, a webcam, and the built-in mic in the same afternoon.

If Automatic guesses wrong, pick the device by name. Choose the built-in microphone for a laptop-only session. Choose the desk mic when you sit at a desk with one plugged in.

Then confirm that macOS hears you:

1. Open **Apple menu > System Settings > Sound**.
2. Under **Input**, select the same device you picked in Dictation.
3. Speak at normal volume and watch the input level. If the meter stays flat, the Mac is not getting sound from that device.

If an app still cannot hear you, open **System Settings > Privacy & Security > Microphone** and make sure the app is switched on. Quit and reopen the app after you change the switch.

A steady desk mic beats AirPods that drop in and out. Normal speaking volume works better than whispering, because a quiet voice gives the recognizer less to work with. If you dictate in a shared office, a close mic cuts down on the room noise that gets transcribed by mistake.

## Set a dictation shortcut you can press

Apple's page does not name a default shortcut. It says to press the Microphone key if your keyboard has one, use the Dictation keyboard shortcut, or choose **Edit > Start Dictation**. Some third-party guides say a double press of Control or Fn is the default. Apple does not confirm that, so check your own Shortcut setting instead of trusting a blog post.

To change the shortcut:

1. Open **Keyboard > Dictation**.
2. Click the **Shortcut** pop-up menu and choose an option from the list.
3. For a combination that is not listed, choose **Customize**, then press the keys you want. Apple's example is **⌥ Z**.

Pick a shortcut you can press with one hand. Avoid keys that another app already uses for something you do often. A conflict fires the wrong action, and that is harder to notice than a missing word.

| Start method | Where it lives | When to use it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Microphone key | Keyboards that have one | Fastest, with no setup |
| Dictation shortcut | Shortcut pop-up in Keyboard > Dictation | Daily use in any text field |
| Edit > Start Dictation | Menu bar, in the app you are using | When you forget the shortcut |

## Dictate, stop, and check the text

This is the loop you repeat every time.

1. Click into the field where the words belong. A note, a mail draft, or a document works. A search box does not.
2. Press your shortcut or the Microphone key.
3. Speak in full phrases. "Send Priya the revised invoice before Friday" comes out as one clean sentence. Single words with long gaps do not.
4. Stop with **Escape**, the Microphone key, or the shortcut.
5. Read the text once. Fix names, numbers, and dates before you send anything.

Apple sets two limits here. Dictation has no time limit on length. It also stops on its own after 30 seconds without detected speech. A long pause ends the session, so press the shortcut again and keep going.

On Apple silicon Macs, you can keep typing while dictation runs. That helps when you dictate the main idea and type a name or a link by hand.

The most common mistake is starting dictation before the cursor is in the field. The Mac hears you, and the words go nowhere useful. Click first, then start.

Another mistake is dictating into Notes, then copying the result into Slack or Mail. That adds a step and a second place to proofread. Dictate in the field you will send from.

## A quick checklist before you dictate at work

Run through this list once when you set up a new Mac, and again when you change headsets or move to a new desk.

1. Dictation is on under **Keyboard > Dictation**, and your language is correct.
2. **Microphone source** names the device you actually use, not Automatic if Automatic has guessed wrong before.
3. The input meter under **Sound > Input** moves when you speak.
4. Your shortcut does not clash with an app you use every day.
5. You have run one test sentence in Notes and read it back.

If all five pass, the problem is almost always the field you are typing into, not the Mac.

## Limits to know before you rely on it

Five limits catch people off guard.

- **Voice Control blocks standard Dictation.** When Voice Control is on, standard Dictation is not available. Voice Control lives under **System Settings > Accessibility**. Turn it off if you want Dictation, or use Voice Control to dictate instead. Choose one tool for each job, not both at once.
- **No search boxes.** General Dictation is for messages and notes. Type your search terms.
- **Languages and regions vary.** Dictation is not available in every language or region. Check Apple's feature availability page for yours.
- **The enhanced model is narrow.** In macOS 27, the on-device model that improves spelling, punctuation, and capitalization is English only. It needs a Mac with an M3 chip or later and at least 12GB of unified memory. Check your chip and memory under **Apple menu > About This Mac** before you count on it.
- **Pauses end sessions.** Thirty seconds of silence stops dictation.

If you are not sure what your Mac has, open About This Mac first. That takes ten seconds and saves a lot of guessing.

## How accurate is Mac dictation?

Speech is faster than typing for raw text. The final text still needs a read. That is the honest summary of the research I checked.

The most useful study is a 2016 arXiv paper by Ruan, Wobbrock, Liou, Ng, and Landay. It tested short messages on an iPhone 6 Plus, not on a Mac. In English, speech ran at 153 words per minute. The keyboard ran at 52, so speech was 2.93 times faster.

Speech also produced fewer corrected errors during entry. Corrected error rates were 5.30% for speech and 11.22% for the keyboard. Uncorrected errors in the final text went the other way, at 1.30% for speech and 0.79% for the keyboard. Speech got the words out faster and left slightly more mistakes behind.

Here is what that means in practice. At the study's keyboard rate of 52 words per minute, a 150-word email takes about 2.9 minutes to type. At 153 words per minute, the same email takes about one minute to speak. The read-through still takes time, so budget for it.

The authors say their results came from ideal lab conditions. They also say real-world performance needs more study. Treat the numbers as a benchmark, not a promise for your office.

A separate poll points to everyday use. A June 2024 Eureka Surveys poll of 36,501 participants found that 29.6% use voice dictation for texting most or all of the time. The poll's method is not clear, so read it as a rough signal.

## Mac dictation not working: fixes in order

Work through these in order. Most failures are one of them.

**Nothing appears when you speak.** Open **System Settings > Sound > Input** and watch the meter. Switch to the built-in microphone, speak, and see whether the meter moves. Then reconnect AirPods or your desk mic.

**Dictation is off or grayed out.** Confirm the switch is on under **Keyboard > Dictation**. A managed Mac can lock that setting. On a work machine, ask whoever manages it.

**It works in Notes but fails in another app.** Test in Notes first. If only one app fails, the problem is likely that app's text field. Click into a plain field in that app and try again.

**Voice Control is on.** Standard Dictation does not run while Voice Control is on. Turn Voice Control off under **System Settings > Accessibility**, then try again.

**Names come out wrong.** Check the language first. Then fix names by hand on the read-through. Dictation gives you a first draft, and names are where first drafts fail most often.

**It stops mid-sentence.** Thirty seconds of silence ends the session. Press the shortcut again and carry on.
