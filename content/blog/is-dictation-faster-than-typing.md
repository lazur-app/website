Yes, dictation is faster than typing for getting a first draft onto the screen. In a well-known lab test, people spoke English at 153 words per minute and typed on a phone keyboard at 52, a gain of about 2.9 times for speech.

Speed on the way in is only part of the story. Hesitation, corrections and errors all take time too, and how much they cost depends on you and the task. This guide walks through the numbers, where each method wins, and a simple test you can run on your own work.

## What the lab numbers say about dictation speed

Most of the hard numbers on this question come from one study. Ruan, Wobbrock, Liou, Ng, and Landay compared speech and keyboard entry for short messages on an iPhone 6 Plus. The paper appeared on arXiv in 2016 and was later published.

Participants copied phrases that someone else had written. English speech came in at 153 words per minute. The keyboard managed 52. The team also tested Mandarin, where speech ran at 123 words per minute against 43 for the keyboard. Both languages showed a speed-up of about 2.9 times.

| Input method | English | Mandarin | Where the number comes from |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Speech | 153 WPM | 123 WPM | Ruan et al., arXiv 2016 |
| Phone keyboard | 52 WPM | 43 WPM | Ruan et al., arXiv 2016 |
| Speech speed-up | 2.93 times | 2.87 times | Ruan et al., arXiv 2016 |

Read the setup closely. Copying is the easiest kind of writing. You do not compose, choose a tone, or fix a sentence halfway through. Those are the moments where most real work happens. The study shows a ceiling for raw entry speed. It does not show your average workday.

The test also used a phone. A touchscreen keyboard is a different tool from a full-size keyboard on your desk. Expect the gap at your desk to differ from the gap in the study. Test it instead of assuming.

For a wider view of typing, a study from Aalto University analyzed 136 million keystrokes from 168,000 volunteers. It describes how people really type. It does not test dictation, so use it as a baseline for typing behavior only.

## Why the gap shrinks once you count editing

Speed on the first pass is one number. Total time to a finished message is another. The Laxis summary of the Ruan study breaks down where speech time went. About 39% went to speaking. About 31.5% went to hesitation. About 23% went to the recognizer processing the audio, and about 8.5% went to corrections. Those shares come from a secondary summary, so check the paper before you quote them.

Hesitation is the number to watch. When you stop mid-sentence to find a word, the clock keeps running and nothing appears on screen. Typists pause too, but a pause at the keyboard looks like thinking. A pause while speaking feels like dead air, and you notice it more.

Errors are the second cost. In the same summary, speech left more errors in the final text. The figures were 0.55% of characters wrong for speech and 0.35% for the keyboard. After correction, the rates were 3.93% for speech and 4.72% for the keyboard. Both are small, and neither is zero. Proof anything that will leave your desk.

For a deeper look at error types, read [dictation errors vs typing errors on Mac](/blog/dictation-errors-vs-typing-errors).

## A simple formula for time to send

Use one formula. Time to send equals drafting time plus fixing time. Dictation wins when its speed advantage on drafting is bigger than the extra time it adds to fixing.

Take a message you send often. Estimate how long it takes to type. Then estimate how long it takes to speak it and fix it. If speaking plus fixing is shorter, dictation wins for that message.

The ratio of about 2.9 is a starting point, not a promise. Your voice, your microphone, your room and your editing habits all move the result. Two people can get very different answers from the same tool.

## Edge cases that change the answer

Some situations shift the math in ways the headline number does not show.

- **Long documents.** A short reply is easy to dictate and check. A ten-page report has more places for an error to hide. Dictate section by section, and read each section back before you move on.
- **Mixed languages.** The Mandarin arm of the study still favored speech, at 123 words per minute against 43. If you switch languages mid-message, expect more fixing, and check the names and terms that changed.
- **Read-only text.** In Word, the Dictate button can be grayed out in read-only documents. You cannot dictate into a locked file, so type the change, or ask the document owner to make it editable first.
- **Open offices.** Background chatter lowers accuracy. Microsoft says noise and weak microphones both hurt. A pair of earbuds with a decent mic often fixes more than a faster tool does.
- **Heavy formatting.** Bold text, tables and tracked changes are slow to dictate. Draft the words by voice, then format with the keyboard and the menus.

None of these rule dictation out. They tell you where to slow down and where to type.

## Where dictation is faster in everyday work

| Task | Why speaking helps | What to watch |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Email replies | You already know the answer | Names, dates and figures need a read |
| Slack and chat | Short, conversational sentences | Punctuation words and emoji |
| Tickets and specs | Narrative text, not syntax | Code blocks still need typing |
| Meeting follow-ups | Context is fresh in your head | Owners and due dates |
| First drafts | Thinking out loud comes naturally | Structure and headings need a pass |

The strongest case is the first draft of anything you already understand. You know the point. Speech gets the words onto the screen without the friction of finding each key. You can then move to the keyboard for the final pass.

Email is the clearest win. Most replies are a few sentences that answer one question. Saying the answer is often quicker than composing it in your head and then typing it. Keep the reply short, and speak it in one or two chunks.

Tickets and specs work for the same reason. The text is narrative. You describe what should happen and why. Snippets of code still belong on the keyboard.

## Where typing still wins

Typing has clear advantages in four places.

- **Code and symbols.** Brackets, regex and exact identifiers are slow to say and easy to mis-transcribe. Type them.
- **Rearranging text.** Moving paragraphs, tightening a sentence in the middle of a paragraph or cutting a list is quicker with a cursor and a shortcut. Most dictation tools are built for adding text, not moving it.
- **Single-word replies.** For "Sounds good," tapping keys beats starting a voice session.
- **Loud rooms and poor audio.** Microsoft says accuracy drops with background noise, weak internet and low-quality microphones. If your room is loud, the speed gain disappears quickly.

Hybrid is the normal case. Speak the paragraph. Type the tweak. That split keeps most of the speed without the cleanup pain.

## How to test it on your own work

Lab numbers do not tell you about your inbox. Run your own test over a few days of ordinary messages.

- Start with a typing baseline. Run the [typing speed test](/tools/typing-speed-test) and write down your words per minute.
- Pick five real messages from your inbox or Slack. Real messages teach you more than invented ones.
- Type two of them. Start a timer when the compose box opens. Stop it when you press Send.
- Dictate three. Speak each message in one or two chunks, then fix it. Time the fixing separately.
- Compare total time per message. Ignore words per minute on the transcript.
- Repeat for a few days. Early attempts run slow while you learn the tool.

If you want a quicker check, try the [speaking vs typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test). It gives you a rough read in less time. The real answer still comes from your own messages.

## Built-in dictation has limits you should plan around

Built-in tools do the job, but they come with limits. Plan for them before you judge the speed.

**Microsoft Word.** Microsoft says dictation is for Microsoft 365 subscribers. It needs a microphone and a reliable internet connection. In Word on a Mac, Option+F1 starts dictation. Word supports voice commands for punctuation, editing, formatting, lists, comments and symbols. The command list varies by language.

**iPhone.** Turn dictation on in Settings > General > Keyboard > Enable Dictation. Tap the microphone key to speak and the keyboard key to type again. Say "new paragraph" or "new line" for breaks. There is no voice command for deleting mistakes. Tap to select the wrong text, then dictate the fix.

The lesson is simple. Many tools handle adding text well and editing text poorly. Budget keyboard time for the fix, and you will stop being surprised by the total.

## Mistakes that make dictation look slower than it is

- **Judging day one.** The first session is slow for most people. Give it a week of real use.
- **Dictating before you know the point.** Rambling costs more than typing one clear sentence.
- **Measuring the wrong thing.** Words per minute on a transcript tells you about speech, not about finished writing.
- **Speaking one long run.** Long monologues make errors harder to find. Short chunks are easier to check.
- **Blaming the tool for a bad room.** A loud room or a weak laptop mic hurts accuracy. Fix the setup before you judge the speed.
- **Skipping the names check.** Names, figures and product terms are where dictation most often goes wrong. Check them before you send.

## Setup that keeps the speed

Small changes protect the gain.

Use a headset or a decent external microphone, and sit in a quiet room. Microsoft ties accuracy to microphone quality and background noise, so this is the cheapest upgrade you can make.

Learn the punctuation words for your app. On iPhone, "new paragraph" and "new line" save you a trip to the keyboard. Word lists its own commands by language, so check the list for yours.

Speak in full sentences, then pause to check. A two-sentence chunk is easy to scan. A five-minute monologue is not.

Keep the keyboard for the last pass. Use it for code, symbols and any text that needs to move. That split is the one that keeps the speed.
