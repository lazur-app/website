A day with Lazur in Slack, email and Cursor means one voice assistant follows your work across three Mac apps. You hold a key, ask about what is on screen, and hear the answer or see text typed at your cursor.

This guide walks through one example workday. It covers a Slack thread, a customer email, a Cursor bug, and the handoff back to Slack. Each step lists the keys, the request, and what you still check yourself. The figures come from Microsoft's 2025 workplace research and from the Cursor and Lazur pages listed in the sources.

## Why Slack, email, and Cursor fill up one workday

Work rarely stays in one app. Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index found that the average worker receives 117 emails a day. Most of those emails are skimmed in under 60 seconds. The same research counts 153 Teams messages per weekday for the average worker. Teams is Microsoft's chat app, so read that number as a rough sign of chat load, not a Slack count.

Microsoft also reports that employees are interrupted every two minutes during core work hours. Its count of 275 interruptions a day uses a 24-hour base. The two-minute figure uses an 8-hour base. Do not combine them into one claim.

| Signal | Figure | Scope |
|---|---|---|
| Emails received per day | 117 | Average worker, Microsoft 2025 |
| Teams messages per weekday | 153 | Average worker, rolling 28-day sums |
| Interruptions per day | 275 | Meetings, emails, or chats, 24-hour base |

These are averages from a survey of 31,000 full-time knowledge workers in 31 markets, combined with Microsoft 365 telemetry. Your week will look different. The point is that a day across three busy apps is normal. A tool that helps you move between them has real work to do.

Use the table below as the plan for the rest of this guide. Each row is one step in the example day.

| Time | App | What you ask | What you still check |
|---|---|---|---|
| 9:10 AM | Slack | What was decided, and what is still open | Names, dates, and promises |

## Set up the shortcuts and permissions first

Do this before your first meeting, not in the middle of a thread.

1. Download the Mac app from [/download](/download). The app runs on macOS, on Apple silicon and Intel Macs. Windows is planned, not available.
2. Open the app and start the trial. The Pro trial lasts 3 days and needs no credit card. It starts the first time you open the app, so open it when you are ready to use it.
3. Grant microphone access and screen access when macOS asks. You can review both later in System Settings > Privacy & Security.
4. Learn three actions. Hold ⌥⌘ (Option and Command together) to ask about what is on screen. Hold fn to dictate text at the cursor. Use Command Mode to ask for a reply, a shorter version, or a different tone.
5. Run a test in a window with no private data. Ask a plain question and confirm the answer comes out loud.

Two details trip people up. The site also calls Command Mode “Intent Mode,” so search both names when you read the docs. The screen is read only while you hold the shortcut, so keep sensitive windows closed when you are not asking.

## Slack: turn a long thread into a decision

It is 9:10 AM. A launch thread in a public channel grew overnight. There are dozens of replies, and nobody has written down what was decided. You could reread everything, or you could ask for a summary and check it against the thread.

1. Scroll until the decision and the open questions are both on screen. The screen is read as one screenshot, so anything scrolled out of view does not count.
2. Hold ⌥⌘ and ask: “What did the team decide, and what is still open? Name the person each open item is waiting on.”
3. Listen to the answer. If a name is missing, ask, “Which message said that?” Then scroll to that message and ask again.
4. Click into the channel's message box. Hold fn and dictate the update in plain sentences. Say the labels out loud, then fix any formatting you do not like.
5. Read the draft before you post. Check every name and every date. Slack usually sends when you press Return, so read first.

Two edge cases matter here. If the decision lives in a linked document, the voice assistant only sees the page on your screen. Open the document first. A public channel can also reach people beyond the launch team. Post the summary only if it is safe for everyone who can see the channel.

The common mistake is asking for a summary of a thread you have not read. The answer is only as good as what you put on screen. If the summary states a decision you did not see, scroll back and check it before you repeat it to the team.

## Email: draft a customer reply without rewriting the thread

It is 11:30 AM. A customer asked three questions in one Gmail thread. One question asks for a date, and you do not want to guess. A reply is a commitment. Once the customer reads “next Thursday,” that phrase becomes the deadline.

1. Keep the whole thread visible, and scroll to the newest message.
2. Hold ⌥⌘ and ask: “List the three questions. Which one needs a date?”
3. Find the date yourself. Check your calendar or project tracker. The voice assistant only knows what is on screen, so it cannot see your release plan unless that plan is open.
4. Put the cursor in Gmail's reply box and use Command Mode. Say: “Reply to this. Answer the timeline question with the date I confirmed. Say we are checking the integration. Keep it under 120 words and calm.” Put the real date into the request, not a placeholder.
5. Read the reply. Check the greeting, the name, the recipient list, and any attachment. Press ⌘ Return to send if you use Gmail's shortcut.

Command Mode writes its output into the app you are already in. The draft lands in the compose box, so you do not paste it from a separate window.

Three mistakes show up often in email:

- Asking for a reply that “sounds confident.” That invites promises. Give it the facts, then tell it what not to promise, such as a ship date you have not confirmed.
- Forgetting the recipients. A reply to a long thread can go to more people than you expect. Check the To and Cc lines before you send.
- Taking the first draft. Ask for a shorter version with “Shorten this to three sentences,” or change the tone, then compare the two drafts.

## Cursor: explain an error and write the prompt

It is 2:00 PM. A login test fails in Cursor after a refactor. You want the cause before you ask an agent to change anything. A vague prompt wastes the agent's time and yours.

1. Put the failing test output and the function on screen. Close any .env file, token file, or dashboard that shows keys. A screenshot shows everything that is visible.
2. Hold ⌥⌘ and ask: “Why does this test fail? Which line can receive a null token?” The answer comes out loud. Ask again for a line number if you need one.
3. Write the task. Click into Cursor's chat box, hold fn, and dictate: “Refactor this to use the existing error service. Keep the public API the same. Add a test for a failed login with an expired token.”
4. Read the prompt. Fix any wrong file or function name, because dictation can get names wrong. Send it yourself. The voice assistant does not click send or edit files for you.
5. Review the diff and run the tests. If the diff touches more files than the prompt named, stop and ask why before you accept it.

The guide on voice writing for Lazur's Command Mode is blunt about secrets: “Do not speak API keys. Treat the mic like Slack.” Treat anything you say as something another person could hear. Treat anything on screen as something the screenshot can carry.

## Cursor in Slack: hand the task to a cloud agent

Cursor's Slack integration is separate from any voice tool. You set it up in Cursor and Slack. A voice assistant can help you draft the message, but it does not run the agent for you.

Cursor's docs list four setup steps:

1. Click Connect next to Slack in the Cursor integrations dashboard.
2. Install the Cursor app in your Slack workspace.
3. Return to Cursor. Connect a repository provider, pick a default repo, enable usage-based pricing, and confirm the privacy settings.
4. Mention @Cursor with a prompt in a channel, or message Cursor directly in a DM.

Once it is set up, the behavior is predictable:

- Each top-level mention starts its own agent.
- In a thread, @Cursor adds a follow-up to that work. Writing @Cursor agent starts a separate agent instead.
- Reactions show status: ⏳ running, ✅ completed, ❌ failed.
- When a run finishes, you get a Slack notification with an option to view the pull request in GitHub.

At 3:30 PM, you finish the handoff. Hold fn in the Slack thread and dictate the follow-up: “@Cursor in this thread, add the failed-login test on the auth branch, and keep the public API the same.” Post it. Watch the reaction, then open the pull request from the notification.

Check these limits before your team relies on the setup:

- Team Pools require an Enterprise plan.
- Channel settings only apply to public channels.
- Privacy Mode (Legacy) is not supported.
- Cloud Agents need temporary code storage while they run.

Check Cursor's pricing page for usage costs, because usage-based pricing is part of setup.

The common mistake is assuming the agent knows the whole Slack conversation. Put the key facts in the mention itself. Link the thread only if your team allows it, and remember that private repository access is a team decision, not a personal one.

## What leaves your Mac when you use voice AI

Voice tools send some data to outside providers. The privacy policy for the Mac app, last updated October 30, 2026, describes three paths:

- Dictation audio goes to Groq and OpenAI for transcription. The policy says the audio is not stored.
- When you ask a question, your voice, one screenshot, selected text, the front app's name, and local time go to Google Gemini. The policy says the screenshot and audio are not stored.
- Memory, clipboard slots, and job reports stay on the Mac. Dictation and command history stays on the Mac by default. Cloud history is optional and can be deleted.

The policy also says the app “never records or watches in the background,” and that screen content is captured only when you ask.

The home page says “Nothing stored.” The privacy policy is more specific, and it describes history that is kept on the Mac by default. When the two disagree, trust the policy, and read it before you decide what to dictate.

Practical rules for a day like this:

- Treat each hold as a send. The shortcut tells the app to read your screen or your voice.
- Close secrets before you hold ⌥⌘. That includes tokens, passwords, and customer account numbers.
- Check your employer's rules before you send customer email, employee records, or private repository code to any AI provider.
- Remember that a screenshot of a Slack thread also shows other people's messages.

## Mistakes people make in a day like this

1. Asking about content that is off screen. Scroll to it first, then hold the shortcut.
2. Dictating passwords, API keys, or personal data into any app.
3. Sending the first draft. Read it, then check names, dates, and recipients.
4. Expecting the tool to click send, merge a pull request, or edit a file. It explains and drafts. You act.
5. Starting the trial before permissions are set. Start the trial when you can test a real request right away.
6. Writing vague Cursor prompts. Name the file, the function, and the result you expect.
