## The core difference between speaking and typing

Speaking is faster for raw output, averaging 130 to 150 words per minute compared to 51.56 words per minute for typing. While the raw speed of your voice is higher, the total time to reach a finished document often favors a hybrid approach.

Many people assume that because they can talk faster than they can type, dictation will automatically make them more productive. This logic ignores the reality of composition. When you type, your fingers often move at the speed of your thought process. You pause to structure sentences and refine your phrasing in real time. When you speak, you can output words much faster than you can organize them. This often leads to rambling drafts that require significant editing later.

## Understanding the 2.93x speed claim

You have likely heard that dictation is nearly three times faster than typing. This figure comes from a 2016 study that compared voice input to smartphone touchscreen typing. The study measured transcription tasks where participants read text aloud. It did not measure the creative process of writing a report or an email from scratch. If you are transcribing a document, voice is indeed much faster. If you are writing a complex email to a client, the speed advantage shrinks because you must think about what to say next.

## When to use dictation for work

Dictation shines when you need to capture ideas quickly. It removes the physical bottleneck between your brain and the screen. Use dictation for:

*   First drafts of emails or memos where structure matters less than getting ideas down.
*   Brainstorming sessions where you need to log thoughts before they fade.
*   AI prompts where the model can interpret your intent even if your phrasing is slightly messy.

By using your voice to fill the screen with raw content, you bypass the friction of staring at a blank cursor. This is where tools like Lazur help. You can hold the fn key on your Mac to dictate clean, formatted text directly into your active app. This lets you turn your spoken thoughts into text immediately.

## When to stick with your keyboard

Typing is superior for tasks that require precision and structure. You should use your keyboard for:

*   Editing and proofreading documents where you need to move the cursor to specific characters.
*   Technical writing that includes code, formulas, or complex formatting like brackets and indents.
*   Short, punchy messages where you need to hit a specific tone that is easier to control with your fingers.

Surgical changes are much faster with a keyboard. If you need to swap one word in a sentence or adjust the alignment of a paragraph, trying to do so by voice is often slower than using your keys. The physical feedback of a keyboard helps you maintain focus on the structure of your document.

## The hybrid workflow strategy

Most high-performers use a hybrid workflow. They dictate their first draft to get the core message out quickly. Then, they switch to their keyboard to refine, edit, and format the text. This method combines the raw speed of speaking with the surgical precision of typing. It reduces the time spent staring at a blank screen while keeping your final output polished and accurate.

## Managing the cleanup tax

Dictation is not perfect. Even with modern AI, you will encounter errors. A common estimate is that you will spend about 25 percent of your total drafting time correcting and formatting the text you just dictated. If you do not account for this time, you might find that your total time-to-sendable-message is not actually shorter than if you had typed it from the start. Always evaluate your work based on the time it takes to send the message, not just the time it takes to write the first draft.

## Measuring your own performance

Do not take these averages as absolute rules for your own work. Your typing speed is likely different from the average, and your speaking style may be more or less concise than others. Test both methods for a week. Use [our typing speed test](/tools/typing-speed-test) to establish a baseline for your keyboard skills. Then, track how long it takes you to draft a standard email using dictation versus typing. You will quickly see which method wins for your specific tasks.

## Common mistakes in dictation

One common mistake is trying to dictate complex technical content. If you are working in Excel or writing code, dictation commands for symbols like brackets or specific operators can feel clunky. You might find yourself saying "open bracket, x equals y, close bracket" which is far slower than simply typing the code. Reserve dictation for prose and use your keyboard for technical syntax.

Another mistake is not using a dedicated tool for dictation. Using the built-in system tools is fine, but having a dedicated assistant that understands your screen context can help you manage your workflow more effectively. ## The role of cognitive load

Typing provides micro-pauses that help you structure your thoughts. When you type, your brain has a moment to catch up with your fingers. Dictation encourages a more free-flowing, stream-of-consciousness style. This can be a benefit for creative writing, but a hindrance for technical or formal documents. If you find your dictated drafts are too wordy, try to speak in shorter, more deliberate bursts. This will lead to cleaner text and reduce your total cleanup time.
