Enhanced Dictation now goes by the plain name Dictation, and Apple's current Mac Help pages do not use the old name. The requirements depend on which feature you mean. Standard Dictation runs on Apple silicon Macs for supported languages, while the more advanced English model needs an M3 or later Mac with at least 12GB of unified memory.

If you searched this phrase, you probably want one of three answers. Will my Mac run it? Which languages work? Does my speech leave the Mac? This guide answers all three. I checked each claim against Apple's pages and a few third-party sources, and I note where the sources disagree. I did not test these settings on a physical Mac, so treat the checks below as a way to confirm your own setup.

## What happened to Enhanced Dictation?

Older guides and forum posts talk about a setting called Enhanced Dictation. Apple's current dictation help page does not use that name. It has no checkbox by that name either. You manage dictation in System Settings now.

MacPaw's how-to article says Enhanced Dictation was renamed in macOS Catalina. Some other sites say Apple removed the feature. I could not confirm a removal on any Apple page, so I would not plan around that claim. What matters is what your Mac shows today.

To look, open Apple menu > System Settings > Keyboard, then find Dictation on the right. If you see a Dictation switch and a language menu, the feature is on your Mac. The old name does not matter.

## Mac requirements by chip and memory

There are two tiers, and mixing them up causes most of the confusion.

The first tier is standard Dictation. Apple's Sonoma guide says that on Apple silicon, dictation requests are processed on your device for supported languages. It adds that no internet connection is required. That guide covers macOS 14.0, so check it against the version on your Mac.

The second tier is the advanced dictation model. Apple's current Mac Help page says it is available in English on Mac models with M3 and later and at least 12GB of unified memory. It improves spelling, punctuation and capitalization with Apple Intelligence in macOS 27.

Use this table to match your Mac to the right tier.

| Mac type | Standard Dictation | Where speech is processed | Advanced English model |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intel Mac | Yes, for supported languages | Sent to Apple | No, needs M3 or later |
| M1 or M2 Mac | Yes, for supported languages | On the device | No, needs M3 or later |
| M3 or later with 8GB | Yes, for supported languages | On the device | No, needs at least 12GB |
| M3 or later with 12GB or more | Yes, for supported languages | On the device | Yes, English, in listed regions |

Two examples make the table easier to use. Say you have an M3 MacBook Air with 8GB. Standard Dictation works in your languages. The advanced English model does not appear, because Apple sets the memory floor at 12GB.

Now say you have an M1 Pro with 16GB. Standard Dictation works, but the advanced model still does not appear. Apple names M3 or later as the chip requirement, and M1 sits below that line.

Storage is the other constraint. Apple's requirements page says M3 or later Macs with at least 12GB need up to 14 GB for Apple Intelligence on the device. Other eligible Macs need up to 8 GB. Eligible Macs start at M1. So an M1 Mac can run Apple Intelligence, but Apple's dictation page still sets M3 as the bar for the advanced model.

Do not read the table as a rule for every dictation feature. Apple's pages describe these two tiers. They do not say that all dictation needs Apple silicon.

To find out which side of the Intel line you are on, open Apple menu > About This Mac. An Apple silicon Mac lists a chip name such as M2 or M3 under Chip. An Intel Mac lists a Processor line instead. Note the memory line on the same screen, because the 12GB floor depends on it.

## Intel Macs and where your speech goes

This is the requirement people miss most often. Apple's Sonoma guide says that on Intel-based Macs, utterances are sent to Apple to process your requests. Apple silicon Macs keep supported-language processing on the device.

In practice, an Intel Mac needs an internet connection to dictate. Picture an M2 MacBook Air on a train with Wi-Fi off. Once the speech model is downloaded, it still dictates in a supported language. An Intel Mac in the same seat does not get that.

Privacy is the reason this matters. If you dictate client notes, medical details or draft contracts, know where your audio goes before you start. For a deeper look at what on-device means in practice, read [on-device speech to text on Mac](/blog/on-device-speech-to-text-mac).

## Which languages qualify for dictation on Mac

Apple's macOS 27 feature availability page lists on-device and modeless dictation for about 22 base languages. That count is my tally from the page, not a number Apple prints. Here is the list, in alphabetical order:

Arabic, Cantonese, Danish, Dutch, English, Finnish, French, German, Hebrew, Italian, Japanese, Korean, Malay, Mandarin, Norwegian, Portuguese, Russian, Spanish, Swedish, Thai, Turkish and Vietnamese.

The same page says the speech models must be downloaded. Plan for that before you need dictation on a deadline. Apple does not state the download size on the pages I checked, so this guide does not give one.

Apple also says dictation is not available in all languages or regions, and features may vary. Some third-party sites give other totals, such as 34 or about 25. I did not verify those, so use Apple's list as your reference.

To change the language, open Apple menu > System Settings > Keyboard, then Dictation, and pick a language from the menu. Choose the language you speak most at work, then test it. Switching languages mid-sentence often produces odd output, so pick one language for each session.

One mistake shows up here often. People choose a language other than English and expect the advanced features to follow. They do not. The advanced model is English only.

If you write in two languages, treat each language as its own test. Dictate a paragraph in each one and read the results. A language can appear on Apple's list and still give you rough output for names, jargon or product terms. Add a custom word list in a text tool you already use if that happens often.

## Which regions get the advanced English model

Apple's feature availability page lists the advanced dictation for English only, in nine places: Australia, Canada, India, Ireland, New Zealand, Singapore, South Africa, the United Kingdom and the United States.

If you live outside those places, the advanced model may not show up, even on a qualifying Mac. Check your region with Apple menu > System Settings > General > Language & Region. Compare what you see with Apple's list before you spend an hour troubleshooting.

Managed work Macs add one more check. If your company controls the Mac, the Dictation switch can be locked. Ask your IT team before you assume the feature is broken.

## How to check your Mac before you turn on dictation

Work through these steps in order. Each one rules out a common cause.

1. Click the Apple menu > About This Mac. Write down the chip and the memory.
2. Open System Settings > General > Language & Region. Confirm your region. The advanced English model needs a region on Apple's list.
3. Open Apple menu > System Settings > Keyboard, then Dictation. Turn dictation on and choose your language.
4. Check free storage before you download. Apple lists up to 14 GB for Apple Intelligence on M3 Macs with 12GB, so leave room.
5. Test it. Open Notes, click in a note, press the dictation shortcut shown in the Dictation settings, and read two sentences aloud. Check what appeared.

## What to do when the advanced option is missing

Sometimes the advanced option does not show up on a Mac that seems to qualify. Work through these checks in order.

First, check the macOS version. Apple lists the advanced dictation on its macOS 27 feature availability page, so an older system may not show it. Open Apple menu > System Settings > General > Software Update and install what is available.

Second, check the language. The advanced model is English only, so Dictation must be set to English.

Third, check the region. Apple's list covers nine places. Confirm yours under System Settings > General > Language & Region.

Fourth, check the speech model. Apple says the speech models must be downloaded. If the download stopped, reconnect to Wi-Fi and let it finish.

If all four checks pass, the cause is most likely hardware. An M1 or M2 Mac, or an M3 with 8GB, will not show the option, however tidy the settings look.

## Common mistakes that look like a requirement problem

- **Searching for the old name.** Look for Dictation in Keyboard settings. Current pages do not list a separate Enhanced Dictation checkbox.
- **Thinking an 8GB M3 is unsupported.** It runs standard Dictation. It only misses the advanced English model.
- **Expecting an Intel Mac to work offline.** Apple's Sonoma guide says Intel Macs send speech to Apple.
- **Skipping the download.** A missing speech model looks like a broken feature.
- **Reading an old guide.** The Sonoma guide covers macOS 14.0. The current page covers macOS 27. Match the guide to the version on your Mac.
- **Expecting the advanced model in another language.** It is English only, according to Apple's current page.

## Is dictation worth the setup time?

Setup takes a few minutes. The payoff depends on how fast you speak compared with how fast you type. A 2016 study by Ruan and colleagues measured text entry on an iPhone 6 Plus. English speech entry ran at 153 words per minute, against 52 for the keyboard. That is 2.93 times the keyboard rate.

Two caveats matter. The study used a phone, not a Mac, and it tested short text entry, not your work. It also found more uncorrected errors in the final speech text, at 1.30% against 0.79% for the keyboard. Speech saved correction time in that study, but you still need to read what comes out.

Run the study's rates on a 500-word draft. Speech takes about 3.3 minutes. Typing takes about 9.6 minutes. That is simple arithmetic from the study, not a promise for your Mac. To measure your own speed, try the [speaking vs typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test).

## When Apple's built-in dictation is enough

Apple's dictation is free and already on your Mac. For many people, that is the right answer. Use it if your language is on Apple's list, your Mac runs the speech model, and you need text in a field, not a voice assistant.

Look elsewhere if you need features Apple's settings do not cover. The [Apple Dictation alternatives page](/alternatives/apple-dictation) compares price, privacy and strengths for several apps. Check each app's own site for current pricing, since prices change.
