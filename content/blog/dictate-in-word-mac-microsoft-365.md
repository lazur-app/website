To dictate in Word on Mac, click where you want the text, then press Option F1 (⌥ F1). Dictate needs a Microsoft 365 subscription, a working microphone, and a reliable internet connection.

This guide covers the shortcut, the setup checks, the Microsoft 365 limits that catch people out, and the mistakes that cost time. It ends with what the research says about speech and typing, and where a voice assistant fits in.

## What Dictate needs on your Mac

Start with the subscription. Microsoft's Dictate support page says the feature is only for Microsoft 365 subscribers. Microsoft's Word page for the same article also lists Word 2024 and Word 2021 for Mac. Those two statements do not fully match. Check your version and subscription before you rely on Dictate for real work.

Next, the microphone. The built-in microphone works in a quiet room. Open System Settings > Sound > Input, speak, and watch the level meter move. If you use a headset, select it in that same list before you open Word.

Then the connection. Microsoft says Dictate needs a reliable internet connection. Your speech goes to Microsoft so it can return text. Microsoft also says the service does not store your audio data or your transcribed text.

Test before you draft anything long. Say one sentence and check the result. A bad connection or a muted microphone shows up within seconds. You do not want to find out after a full page.

## How to start Dictate with Option F1

Keyboard first. The shortcut is Option F1. Some keyboards need the Fn key as well. A Microsoft Q&A answer from January 2023 lists Fn + Option + F1 as an alternative. If the shortcut does nothing, try that before you change any system setting.

Prefer the mouse? Go to Home > Dictate and click the Dictate button. It starts the same session.

Run this short test once. Say, "Testing one, two. The budget review moves to Thursday." Read the result before you go on. If the sentence comes out right, your microphone, connection and cursor position all work.

Here is a rule that saves time. Start every session with the cursor in the body of the document. Not in a header, a text box or the Find field. Dictation will type into whatever has focus, and that is rarely what you wanted.

## How to stop Dictate and check what it wrote

Stopping is the step people guess at. Microsoft's Mac instructions do not say clearly how to end a session. Third-party guides say pressing Option F1 again switches dictation off. Confirm that on your own Mac by watching the Dictate button. If the button still looks active, click it.

Once dictation stops, read the text out loud or scroll back through it. Dictated text looks clean at a glance. The errors are often real words in the wrong place. Names, figures, dates and product codes need a second look every time.

## Microsoft 365 limits: Dictate and Transcribe are different features

Most of the confusion comes from two features with similar names. Dictate turns your speech into text while you talk. Transcribe turns an uploaded recording into text after the fact.

| | Dictate | Transcribe |
|---|---|---|
| What it does | Types your speech into the document as you talk | Converts an uploaded audio file to text |
| How to start on Mac | Option F1, or Home > Dictate | Not listed for Mac in Microsoft's support page |
| Platforms stated | Word for Microsoft 365, Word 2024 and Word 2021 (including for Mac) | Word for Microsoft 365 on Windows, commercial tenants |
| Monthly limit | Not stated on the Dictate page | 300 minutes for Microsoft 365, 30,000 for Copilot-licensed users |
| Audio formats | Live speech, nothing to upload | .wav, .mp4, .m4a, .mp3 |

Transcribe carries the numbers people quote. Microsoft 365 subscribers can transcribe up to 300 minutes of uploaded audio per month. Users with a Microsoft Copilot license get 30,000 minutes per month. Microsoft's page does not explain how the monthly limit resets. It also does not mention Personal or Family plans.

Transcribe is the feature that does not help a Mac user right now. Microsoft says it works only in Word for Microsoft 365 on Windows, in commercial tenants. It also works only in the new Microsoft Edge and Chrome. Mac is not mentioned at all. If you need to transcribe a recorded interview on your Mac, plan on a different tool. Check Microsoft's Transcribe page again later, because features move.

The live Dictate limit is less clear. Microsoft's Dictate page does not state a monthly minute cap. It also does not say whether live dictation counts toward the Transcribe cap. Do not build a workflow on a guess about either one.

Here is what that means for planning. If your job depends on turning a recording into a document, test the whole chain before a deadline. Record a short clip, run it through the tool you plan to use, and time the result. If the job depends on live speech, Dictate is the feature to test. Either way, the test takes less time than discovering a limit on the day a file is due.

## Languages, punctuation and accuracy by language

Microsoft lists 15 language and locale entries for Dictate. English appears five times, for Australia, Canada, India, the UK and the US. The rest include Chinese (China), French (Canada and France), German (Germany), Hindi, Italian (Italy), Japanese, Portuguese (Brazil) and Spanish (Spain and Mexico).

Microsoft also lists about 40 preview languages. Arabic, Dutch, Korean and Russian are among them. Microsoft says preview languages may have lower accuracy or limited punctuation support.

Say you draft a client email in Dutch. You may get usable words with thin punctuation. Budget time for a full proofread before you send it. Do not expect English-level output from a preview language on day one.

Punctuation has its own setting. Microsoft says Auto Punctuation can be turned on or off, if it is available for the language you chose. If commas and full stops look wrong in a supported language, check that setting first.

## Voice commands and fixing mistakes by voice

Dictate is built for getting words onto the page. Treat it as a drafting tool, not an editor. Commands can vary by version and language. Test any spoken command in a scratch document before you rely on it in a real file.

The faster fix is manual. Stop Dictate, then select the mistake with the mouse or the keyboard. Hold ⌥ ⇧ ← to select one word at a time. Type the correction, then restart Dictate.

Here is a worked example. Dictate writes "their" when you meant "there." Stop the session. Double-click the word, type "there," and read the sentence again. If the change breaks something nearby, press ⌘ Z to undo it.

Restarting is where people lose their place. Click back into the spot where the next sentence goes before you press Option F1. Otherwise the new text lands in the middle of the old text.

## When Option F1 does nothing

Work through these checks in order. Most failures show up in the first three.

1. Confirm the cursor is in the document body, not in a header, a text box or the Find field.
2. Try Fn + Option + F1 instead of Option F1.
3. Open System Settings > Privacy & Security > Microphone and make sure Microsoft Word is switched on.
4. Confirm you are signed in with the Microsoft account that holds your Microsoft 365 subscription.
5. Check the connection. Switch networks, or move closer to your router, and try one sentence again.
6. Quit Word completely, reopen it, then choose Help > Check for Updates.

If none of those work, write down which step failed and which Word version you use. That detail makes support faster.

## Mistakes that cost time

- Opening Transcribe for a live meeting or a dictation session. It handles uploaded files, not live speech.
- Assuming Option F1 works on every keyboard. Try Fn + Option + F1 first.
- Starting to speak before the cursor sits where you want the text.
- Dictating in a noisy room, or with the laptop microphone across the desk.
- Skipping the proofread because the text sounded fine while you spoke.
- Expecting a preview language to match the accuracy of English.

## How fast is speech compared with typing?

A 2017 study by Ruan and colleagues compared speech and keyboard entry for short messages on touchscreen phones. It was published in ACM IMWUT in December 2017, after an arXiv posting in 2016. It is not a study of Word, and it does not test Microsoft Dictate.

The numbers are still useful. English speech entry was 2.93 times faster than the keyboard, at 153 words per minute against 52. Corrected error rates were 5.30% for speech and 11.22% for the keyboard. Uncorrected errors ran a little higher for speech, at 1.30% against 0.79%.

Read those numbers as a pattern, not a promise. Speech was faster. People caught and fixed more of their speech errors. Speech also left slightly more errors uncorrected. That last point is why a proofread matters more after dictation than after typing.

Your own speed is what counts. Run the [typing speed test](/tools/typing-speed-test) and the [speaking versus typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test) to get a baseline before you change how you draft. Then time how long the same page takes each way.

## A drafting routine that works in Word

Dictation works well in two passes. First, dictate the rough draft without stopping to fix things. Second, read the draft aloud and fix names, numbers and dates. Do not try to make the first pass perfect. Speech gives you raw words quickly. Editing is a separate job.

For a two-page proposal, keep each session to a few paragraphs. Stop, read, and fix. Then start again from a clean cursor position. Long unbroken sessions make it harder to find where an error began.

Keep a short list of names and terms that Dictate keeps getting wrong. Spell them correctly in a note, then check that list before you send the file. Client names and product codes are the usual offenders.
