Apple dictation is free, built into macOS, and good enough for short notes, Messages replies, and quick Mail edits. AI dictation apps cost money, but they clean up your words, keep your names and terms spelled right, and use one shortcut across many apps.

The right pick depends on where you type. If most of your day runs through Notes and Messages, keep Apple's feature and stop there. If you write in Slack, Gmail in Chrome, Linear, or Cursor, the difference starts to cost you time.

## What Apple dictation does on a Mac right now

Apple dictation is a setting, not a separate app. Open System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation, then turn it on. You can start it with the Microphone key, the Dictation shortcut you chose, or Edit > Start Dictation in the app you are using. You stop it with Escape, the Microphone key, or the same shortcut.

Two limits matter most. Apple's Mac guide says you can dictate text of any length with no timeout. It also says dictation stops by itself when it hears no speech for 30 seconds. A long pause while you think ends the session. Restart it with the shortcut and keep going.

Apple's Keyboard settings describe general text dictation as "processed on your device and not sent to Siri servers." The guide does not say whether dictation needs an internet connection, so do not assume either way. If offline use matters to you, turn Wi-Fi off for a minute and test it before you rely on it.

Two more limits trip people up. Standard Dictation is unavailable while Voice Control is on. It is also not available in every language or region. If you switch between Voice Control and dictation often, read [Voice Control vs Dictation on Mac](/blog/voice-control-vs-dictation-mac) to see how the two conflict.

Punctuation is the other sticking point. On older macOS versions, you speak punctuation out loud, such as "comma" or "new paragraph." Expect that habit to fade only on the newer models covered below.

## What changes with macOS 27 and Siri AI

Apple's newer releases narrow the gap. They do it only on newer hardware.

In macOS 27, Mac Dictation uses an on-device model to improve spelling, punctuation and capitalization. Apple says this works in English on Mac models with M3 or later and at least 12GB of unified memory. To check your chip and memory, choose Apple menu > About This Mac.

Apple's June 8, 2026 newsroom post on Siri AI says it brings "a major boost in accuracy with systemwide dictation." It says dictation output now handles capitalization, punctuation and formatting as you speak. That applies only to products that support Apple's most advanced on-device model. The footnote lists Mac with M3 or later and at least 12GB of unified memory, and iPad with M4 or later.

Read the fine print before you plan around it. Apple says these features arrive as a beta later this year. English is the launch language. Siri AI is not initially available on iOS in the EU, and it is not available in China yet. Apple's newsroom also lists Apple Intelligence on iPhone 16 models or later, which is a broader list than the advanced dictation hardware. The two scopes differ, so check the exact requirement for your device.

On iPhone, Digital Trends reports that a more advanced dictation system is off by default during the beta. You turn it on under Settings > General > Keyboards. That is a secondary report, not an Apple help page, so confirm it on your own phone.

What this means for you: a Mac with an M1 or M2 chip, or an Intel Mac, does not meet the stated hardware requirement. You keep the standard dictation you have now. Check the requirement before you decide the gap is closed.

## What AI dictation apps add on top

AI dictation apps are a different kind of product. Speech recognition is only the starting point. The app then cleans up the text, applies your custom words, and places the result where your cursor sits. Many people value that cleanup more than the transcription itself.

Take Wispr Flow as an example, since its pricing and privacy pages are public. Its pricing page lists a Free plan at $0 per month. Pro costs $15 per user per month billed monthly, or $12 per user per month billed annually. Enterprise uses custom annual pricing. The Free plan has "limited words per week," but the page gives no number. Check the pricing page before you decide, because free-plan limits change.

Wispr's privacy page says audio capture starts only on a keypress. It says audio goes to Wispr's servers over encrypted connections (TLS 1.2 or later). Its FAQ says "Transcription always happens in the cloud." The same page says audio is deleted once it is transcribed, unless you turn on Dictation cloud storage. It also has an "Improve the model for everyone" setting that lets Wispr use audio, transcripts and edits to improve its models. You can turn that off.

Notice the difference. Apple describes general text dictation as processed on your device. A cloud-based AI app sends your audio out to a server. Neither choice is wrong. It is a trade you should make on purpose.

## Speed and accuracy: what the lab data shows

Start with a study. Ruan et al., from Stanford, the University of Washington and Baidu, compared speech and keyboard entry on phones. English speech averaged 153 words per minute. A touchscreen keyboard averaged 52. The abstract puts speech at 2.93 times faster. In Mandarin, speech averaged 123 words per minute against 43 for the keyboard, or 2.87 times faster.

Speed came with a tradeoff. Speech had a lower corrected error rate, 5.30% vs 11.22%. Its uncorrected error rate in final text was slightly higher, 1.30% vs 0.79%. In plain terms, speech gets you to a draft faster and fixes more of its own mistakes. A few more wrong words stay in the text if you do not read it back.

Be careful with these numbers. The study used an iPhone 6 Plus and a 2016-era recognizer, Baidu's Deep Speech 2. It measured short messages, and participants copied phrases rather than composing their own text. Treat the figures as direction, not a forecast for your Mac.

Here is some arithmetic from those lab speeds. At 153 words per minute, a 100-word reply takes about 40 seconds to speak. At 52 words per minute, the same reply takes about two minutes to type. Your real time will be longer once you add pauses, fixes and rereads.

Lab speed also does not tell you how your dictation handles your accent, your jargon or your background noise. Only your own test answers that. If you want your own typing and speaking numbers, run the [speaking vs typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test).

## Privacy: where your audio goes

Apple and AI apps handle audio differently. Both have settings you should check once.

On a Mac, Apple's "Improve Siri & Dictation" setting lives at System Settings > Privacy & Security > Analytics & Improvements. Turning it on means agreeing to let Apple store and review a sample of audio recordings and transcripts. You choose that during setup, and you can change it later. Apple says only Apple employees review the recordings. It says the data is tied to a random, device-generated identifier, not your Apple Account or email address.

So the free Apple path can involve sharing, but only if you opt in. Look at that switch once. Then leave it where you want it.

AI apps are more varied. Read each app's privacy page before you pick one, and look for three things: where transcription runs, whether audio is kept, and whether your recordings train the vendor's models.

## Where Apple dictation is enough

Apple dictation is strong for short, low-stakes text. Use it for:

- Quick Notes captures, like a grocery list or a reminder to call someone back.
- Messages replies that run a few sentences.
- Mail replies you will read over before you send anyway.
- First drafts of notes you plan to edit by hand.

Here is a typical case. You need a three-line reply to a colleague. You speak it, check the punctuation, and send. The whole job takes a few seconds of speaking. An AI app would save you little here, and you would pay for the difference.

The simple test: if the text goes out almost unchanged, Apple is enough.

## Where AI dictation apps earn the price

AI dictation pays off when the text must read like writing, not speech. Four cases stand out.

- Slack and Linear updates that include product names, ticket numbers and people's names. A custom dictionary helps keep an unusual surname or a client's company name spelled correctly.
- Prompts for Cursor or VS Code, where you speak full sentences and then want them tidy before you send them.
- Client email that should not read like a voicemail transcript. Speech tends to produce run-on sentences and false starts. Cleanup removes the "um" and the restarts.
- Long dictated drafts that need rewording before they go out.

Take a concrete case. You dictate: "send the revised proposal to Dana and cc the finance team and say the Q3 numbers are final." An older Mac gives you that as one long run-on, with the punctuation you spoke. An AI app can split it into clean sentences and fix the names.

Then do the money math. Wispr's Pro plan costs $12 or $15 per user per month, depending on billing, according to its pricing page. Ask yourself how many minutes of fixing that saves each month. If you correct two sentences a day, the price may not matter much. If you rewrite whole paragraphs, it might pay for itself.

## Mistakes people make when comparing the two

1. **Testing only in Notes.** Notes is the easiest case for Apple. Test in the app where you spend the most time.
2. **Judging macOS 27 features on an older Mac.** The on-device model needs M3 or later and 12GB of memory. Check your hardware first.
3. **Forgetting that Voice Control turns off standard Dictation.** If one stops working, check that setting before you blame the app.
4. **Giving both tools the same shortcut.** Two tools fighting for one key make both look bad.
5. **Trusting lab speed as your speed.** The Ruan et al. study used phone messages and copied phrases.
6. **Assuming free means no sharing.** Apple's sharing is opt-in. AI apps send audio to servers. Read the settings for both.
7. **Counting only the time to a first draft.** Count the time until the text is ready to send.

## How to run your own test in one afternoon

You can settle this in an afternoon with three tasks.

1. Pick three tasks you do this week. A good mix is one Slack update, one client email, and one prompt or document paragraph.
2. Start a timer when you press the Apple dictation key. Stop it when the text is ready to send. Include the time you spend fixing mistakes.
3. Count the fixes. Count each word you change, add or delete.
4. Repeat the same three tasks with your AI app. Keep your wording as close to the same as you can.
5. Compare the totals. If Apple wins on time and fixes, keep it. If the AI app wins by a clear margin, the price is probably worth it.

Read the final text, not just the first pass. The Ruan et al. study found that speech leaves a few more uncorrected errors in final text, so the last read matters. ## Can you keep both?

Yes. Keep Apple dictation on for quick captures. Give your AI app a different shortcut so the two never compete for the same key. Most people who switch do not delete Apple's feature. They make the AI app their everyday tool and let Apple handle the odd Notes capture.

After a macOS update, open System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation and check that the shortcut still does what you expect. Also check that Voice Control is still off if you rely on dictation.
