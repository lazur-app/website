Dictation can help some people with dyslexia or ADHD get words onto a Mac screen faster. The research does not yet show that it improves writing quality for either group, and I found no controlled study that measured dictation for adults or children with ADHD.

## What dictation can and cannot do for dyslexia and ADHD

Dictation replaces the keyboard with your voice. You speak, and the Mac turns your speech into text at the cursor. For some people, that removes the hardest step, which is getting the first sentence down at all.

For dyslexia, the appeal is clear. Spelling and decoding can slow a draft to a crawl. Speech skips the spelling step. It does not fix reading, though. You still have to check what the Mac wrote, and checking is often where the time goes.

For ADHD, the picture is different. Typing can pull your attention into small fixes, like deleting the same typo for the fifth time. Talking can keep a thought moving from start to finish. But dictation does not help with planning, sorting ideas, or the urge to open another tab. It helps with one part of the work.

Think of dictation as a way to get raw material onto the page. It is not treatment, and it does not replace editing.

## Which writing suits dictation best?

Dictation works best when the text is loose and the stakes are moderate. Good examples include a first draft of an email, meeting notes, a rough outline, or a long message to a colleague. You can fix a clumsy sentence later.

It works less well for text where every character matters. Code, tables, formulas, and legal wording are hard to dictate cleanly. Names, product codes, and abbreviations also come out wrong unless you teach them to the system first.

A simple rule helps. If you would be happy to tidy the text in a second pass, dictate it. If the first pass has to be exact, type it, or dictate in short chunks and check each one.

## How do you turn on Dictation on a Mac?

Set this up before you need it. You do not want to hunt through settings in the middle of a draft.

1. Open Apple menu > System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation, then switch Dictation on. If your Mac asks, click Enable.
2. Choose a shortcut from the Shortcut pop-up menu. To use your own keys, choose Customize and press them. Apple's example is Option-Z.
3. Open the Microphone source pop-up menu and pick your input. Automatic listens to the device you are most likely to use for Dictation. If it picks the wrong one, choose the mic yourself.
4. Decide on auto-punctuation. It adds commas, periods, and question marks in supported languages. You can turn it off in the same pane.
5. Test the setup. Open Notes, press your shortcut, and dictate three sentences. Read them back before you judge the result.

Two limits show up in the first session. Dictation stops automatically when it detects no speech for 30 seconds. There is no timeout while you keep talking, so long passages are fine. A short pause to think is fine too. A long silence ends the session, and you have to start again.

Apple's current guide is labeled for macOS 27 Golden Gate. If your menus look different, check your version under Apple menu > About This Mac. Apple also says Dictation is not available in every language or region, and features vary by region.

## What does the dyslexia research show?

The best-supported number is about prevalence, not about tools. A 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis in Brain Sciences pooled 58 studies. It estimated developmental dyslexia at 7.10% among primary school children. Boys came in at 9.22% and girls at 4.66%.

That tells you how many children may need support. It does not tell you whether dictation helps them write better. Those are two different questions, and the second one has far less evidence behind it.

Some newer studies look promising in search results. One 2024 single-case study from the Nordic countries reports productivity gains for most students. A 2026 study with Grade 5 students reports no transfer effect compared with handwriting. I could not open the full papers, so I am not treating either result as a fact. Read the journal pages before you rely on them. Notice the disagreement, too. One points toward gains, and the other points toward little carryover into later work.

Be careful with dyslexia figures you see online. Some advocacy pages cite higher rates than the peer-reviewed estimate. I could not verify those figures from a source I could open. When you quote a rate, name the definition and the population you used.

If you do try dictation for a draft, check the first pass in a fixed order. First, check names and numbers, because speech tools guess at both. Second, check sound-alike words such as "their" and "there", which speech output gets wrong often. Third, read for meaning. Spelling is the part dictation already handled for you, so you can spend your attention on sense.

## What does the ADHD research show?

The ADHD numbers describe how common the condition is. The CDC's ADHD data page reports that 7 million US children aged 3 to 17 have a current ADHD diagnosis. That is 11.7% of that age group, based on the 2024 National Survey of Children's Health.

The same page gives a sex breakdown. In 2022 to 2023 data, the rate was 13% for boys and 7% for girls in that age range. The page does not say whether those figures are current or ever diagnosed. It also gives no age-specific rates. Treat them as rough context, not precise rates.

Now the gap. I searched for controlled studies of dictation for adults or children with ADHD that measured writing output. I could not find one in the sources I could open. A vendor blog says the gap exists, and that is a vendor's claim, so weigh it that way.

What does that mean for you? If you have ADHD and dictation feels right, test it on your own work. A population statistic cannot tell you whether it will help you specifically. Try a short burst of speech, then stop and read it back. If the stop-and-read rhythm keeps you on task, that is a useful result. If the speech keeps running ahead of your thinking, that is useful too.

## How much faster is speech than typing?

The clearest speed numbers come from a study by Ruan and colleagues. They compared speech and keyboard entry for short messages on touchscreen phones, in English and Mandarin. It is not a study of Macs or long documents. It is still the best measured comparison I could check.

| Measure | Speech | Keyboard |
|---|---|---|
| English entry speed (words per minute) | 153 | 52 |
| Errors corrected during entry | 5.30% | 11.22% |
| Errors left in the final text | 1.30% | 0.79% |

English speech entry was 2.93 times faster. Speech also made fewer errors during entry. It left slightly more errors in the final text, and that last row matters most if proofreading is slow for you.

Here is a worked example. Say you need to get a 300-word first draft down. At 52 words per minute, typing takes about 5.8 minutes. At 153 words per minute, speaking takes about 2 minutes. The speed gain is real, but it covers entry only. Editing takes its own time.

Now the leftover errors. At 1.30%, a 300-word dictated draft carries about 4 wrong words into the final text. At 0.79%, the typed version carries about 2. Those are small counts. They still add up across a long report, and a proofreading pass has to catch every one.

Test your own numbers with the [speaking vs typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test) and the [typing speed test](/tools/typing-speed-test). Speaking speed varies from person to person, so your own result is the one to trust.

## Mistakes that make dictation worse

- **Treating the first pass as final.** Speech leaves errors in the text. Build a proofreading pass into every draft, and read it out loud if that helps you catch mistakes.
- **Leaving Voice Control on.** Standard Dictation is not available while Voice Control is on. Apple says you use Voice Control to dictate instead. Check System Settings > Accessibility > Voice Control before you blame Dictation.
- **Using the wrong microphone.** Automatic may pick a device you did not expect. A weak input adds errors, so check the Microphone source each time you change headsets.
- **Ignoring names and jargon.** Mac does not know your team's terms until you teach them. See [how to teach Mac dictation names and jargon](/for/writers/problems/dictation-names-jargon-custom-vocabulary-mac).
- **Speaking punctuation you do not need.** With auto-punctuation on, most commas and periods appear without a spoken command. Learn the explicit commands for the cases it misses. See [Dictation commands for punctuation on Mac](/blog/mac-dictation-punctuation-commands).
- **Dictating without a plan.** Speaking does not give you an outline. Write three or four headings first, then dictate under each one.
- **Judging after one session.** A single test tells you about one topic and one mood. Give it a fair run before you decide.

## Which settings change accuracy?

Two settings matter most on a new Mac.

The first is the on-device English model. Apple says it improves spelling, punctuation, and capitalization in macOS 27. It is available in English on Mac models with M3 or later and at least 12GB of unified memory. If your Mac is older or has less memory, you will not get that model. Check Keyboard settings to see what your version offers.

The second is where your audio goes. Apple's guide does not clearly say whether every Dictation session is processed on the device. It points you to the on-device option in Keyboard settings. Look there, and do not assume privacy from the name.

If you want to compare Apple's option with other tools, read [Apple Dictation alternatives for Mac](/alternatives/apple-dictation). It covers price, privacy, and what each app does best.

## How do you run a two-week dictation test?

Pick one kind of writing before you start. Email replies, meeting notes, or a first draft of a report all work. Mixing types makes the results hard to read.

Spend the first week dictating every first draft. Note the minutes from start to a finished first draft. Count the errors you fix during proofreading. If you draft in Word, read [how to use dictation in Word on Mac](/how-to/use-dictation-in-word) first.

Spend the second week typing the same kind of work. Track the same two things.

At the end, compare the times and the error counts. Then ask a plainer question. Did starting feel easier, or did you spend more time fixing text? Your own answer is the most useful result you will get from this test.
