Typeless is a dictation app that turns speech into clean, polished text on Mac, Windows, Linux, iOS, and Android. Lazur is a Mac voice assistant that also dictates, so choose it when you want help with what is on your screen, not just cleaner text.

This comparison is for people who write all day and want spoken words to look written. Both apps sit at your cursor and turn talk into text. The real split is what happens around that text, and what each app costs you to try. Every number below comes from a vendor page or a published study, and the sources are listed at the end. Where a figure is marketing, this post says so.

## How clean is the text you get back?

Both apps promise more than a raw transcript. Typeless says it removes filler words and repetition. It also says it keeps only the final wording when you correct yourself mid-sentence, according to its [homepage](https://www.typeless.com/). It lists support for more than 100 languages, with automatic detection and mixing, and it says it can translate while you speak.

Here is what cleanup looks like in practice. You say, “Send the deck to Priya, no wait, to Marcus by Friday.” A polished result reads, “Send the deck to Marcus by Friday.” That self-correction case matters most for everyday work, because most of us change our minds while we talk.

Lazur’s dictation runs when you hold fn. It dictates clean, formatted text at the cursor in any app. It also has snippets, a personal dictionary, and clipboard slots. The personal dictionary is the feature people skip, and it saves the most time. Add the names you write often, such as Priya, Marcus, a client company, or a product name. Then the cleanup stops guessing at them.

Polished output carries one risk. Cleanup changes your words, so it can change your meaning too. Test each app with three things: a name, a number, and a link. If the number moves or the link breaks, you will spend more time fixing text than you saved.

Mistake to avoid: judging cleanup on one clean sentence. Use a message that has a correction in the middle, a list of three items, and a name the app might not know. That is where the two apps usually differ. If you dictate in a language other than English, test that too. Check Lazur’s language support inside the app before you rely on it.

## How fast is speech compared with typing?

The best published number comes from a Stanford lab study. Ruan and colleagues measured English speech at 153 words per minute and keyboard entry at 52 words per minute on an iPhone 6 Plus. That is a 2.93 times speedup. The study measured copying text on a phone, not composing new text. The paper also says further study is needed in non-laboratory settings, so treat it as a direction, not a promise. Source: [arXiv:1608.07323](https://arxiv.org/abs/1608.07323).

Typeless makes its own claim on its homepage: 220 words per minute for voice against 45 for a QWERTY keyboard, which it calls “4x faster than typing.” Those are vendor figures, not independent measurements. Read them as marketing, and test your own speed.

Here is the arithmetic for a 300-word email, using the Ruan figures. At 153 words per minute, you speak it in about two minutes. At 52 words per minute, you type it in about six. Add the time you spend on cleanup, and your real saving depends on how many edits you make after each paste. Run your own check with the [speaking versus typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test).

A Twilio survey adds some context. In its August 2026 fieldwork of 2,000 UK adults, 36% said they had used voice dictation to write an email at work. The release does not make clear whether that base is all adults or only workers. Read it as a rough signal that dictation is no longer niche in the UK, not as a precise rate. Source: [Twilio press release](https://www.twilio.com/en-us/press/releases/talk-over-text--over-a-fifth-of-brits-prefer-talking-over-typing).

## Which platforms can you use?

Typeless covers the most ground. Its help FAQ lists macOS, Windows, and Linux on desktop, and iOS and Android on mobile, per its [help FAQs](https://www.typeless.com/help/faqs). If you dictate on a Mac at work and on an iPhone at lunch, one account can cover both.

Lazur is built for the Mac. The homepage says “Built for macOS,” and the app runs on Apple silicon and Intel Macs. Windows is planned, not available. If your team uses Windows laptops, Lazur will not help there today.

For a Mac-only person, the platform row barely matters. The question becomes whether you want the same tool on your phone. Typeless wins that point. Lazur wins if the Mac is where your real work happens, because its screen help only works there.

Edge case: you have a Mac at work and a Windows PC at home. Typeless on both machines gives you one set of cleanup habits. Two apps mean two sets of habits, and you will notice the difference in the text you send.

## Typeless pricing and Lazur trial: what each costs to try

Typeless starts you on a free plan with a limited weekly word allowance. The official pages do not give the number, so you cannot plan around a fixed figure. Track your own count for a week. Its Pro plan costs $12 per member billed yearly, or $30 billed monthly, and it removes the word limit, according to its [pricing page](https://www.typeless.com/pricing). Choosing yearly saves $216 a year against monthly. That is $360 for twelve monthly payments of $30, against $144 for twelve yearly payments of $12.

Lazur has no permanent free plan. New accounts get a one-time short Pro trial with no credit card, per the [Lazur terms](https://www.lazur.app/terms). The trial starts when you first open the app. When it ends, paid features stop until you subscribe. Lazur offers one Pro tier and one Power tier, and both include unlimited dictation. For the current price of each tier, check the [pricing page](/pricing). Prices move, so this post does not quote them.

Practical tip: open Lazur on a day when you will write a lot. The trial clock starts on first open, so opening it on a quiet Sunday uses up trial days you could have spent on real work.

Mistake to avoid: comparing monthly Typeless against yearly Lazur, or the reverse. Compare the same billing period, and count the words or actions you use, not the headline price.

## How long can one dictation run?

Typeless caps each dictation at 9 minutes. The app shows a 60-second countdown on the voice bar at the 8-minute mark. When you hit the limit, the dictation saves to History automatically, according to its [dictation limit page](https://www.typeless.com/help/troubleshooting/dictation-limit). The page does not say which plans the cap applies to, so ask support if you dictate long memos.

For most messages, nine minutes is plenty. A long meeting recap or a draft report is a different story. Split it into parts. Dictate section one, pause, then dictate section two. You keep your flow and avoid losing a long take at the cap.

I could not find a per-dictation limit for Lazur in the pages reviewed for this post. Before you rely on it for long-form work, test a five-minute voice note and see where it stops. Do not assume the answer.

## Privacy: where your audio goes

Privacy is where the two apps need the most careful reading. Typeless says on its homepage that audio is processed securely in real time and never stored in the cloud. It also says dictation history stays on your device. Its Pro plan lists cloud sync, though. Those two statements can both be true, but they do not answer the same question. Before you dictate client names or contract terms, ask the company which data syncs and where.

Lazur’s public description is narrower about timing. It listens and looks at the screen only while you hold the shortcut. When you ask a question, the audio and the screenshot go to AI providers so Lazur can answer. Lazur says they are not stored, and that your content is never used to train AI models.

Two mistakes come up often here. The first is assuming “on device” means nothing leaves your Mac. Check which parts of the work happen on the machine and which go out. The second is assuming a voice app that sees your screen sees only what you intend. Close sensitive windows before you hold the shortcut, the same way you would before a screen share.

## Which one fits your week?

Choose by the work, not the headline. Here are three patterns that tend to hold.

If most of your day is email, docs, and notes, and you dictate on an iPhone too, Typeless fits well. You get polished text on every device, and there is no screen help to learn.

If you live on a Mac and often ask, “Where is this setting?” or “What does this error mean?”, Lazur fits better. Hold ⌥ ⌘ to ask, and Lazur answers out loud. It can also draw a circle and a label around the thing you are looking for. It will not click it for you.

If you spend most of the day in one app, such as Slack or Gmail, both tools can handle it. Run the test below and let the edit count decide.

Worked example. A customer asks for a refund date. You dictate the reply in Typeless, and the filler disappears on the way to the page. Then you hold ⌥ ⌘ in your billing tool and ask Lazur where the date field is. Lazur points at it. It does not fill the field for you. If you need the steps, ask Lazur to walk you through it. It guides you one click at a time and waits for each click.

## A one-week test you can run

Run this before you pay for anything. It takes about 20 minutes a day for a week.

1. Pick five real messages from last week. Use one long message, one short one, one with a name, one with a number, and one with a correction.
2. Dictate each message in Typeless into the app where you would send it. Then repeat with Lazur, in the same app, at a similar time of day.
3. After each paste, count your edits. Count each changed word as one edit, and each deleted sentence as one edit.
4. Time the gap from your first word to the message being sent. Use a timer on your phone.
5. Check the name, the number, and the link in every message.
6. In Typeless, track your weekly word count so you know how close you are to the free limit.

Decide by the lower edit count and the faster time to send. If the results are close, pick the app with the privacy page that fits your client work better.

## Mistakes to avoid when comparing dictation apps

Most poor comparisons share a few habits. Avoid them.

- Testing with one short sentence. Short sentences hide cleanup problems.
- Treating the vendor speed number as your own speed. Test your own speaking pace.
- Forgetting cleanup time. A fast paste that you then fix for five minutes is not fast.
- Ignoring your other devices. A Windows PC or an iPhone can decide the choice on its own.
- Expecting Lazur to act for you. It guides and points. It does not click buttons or operate other apps.

Hold fn to dictate at the cursor in any app. Hold ⌥ ⌘ when you need help with what is on screen, and Lazur answers out loud. Lazur runs on the Mac only, so Windows machines are out of scope for it.
