Messy speech becomes finished writing when you dictate the rough version once, then make one cleanup pass. Remove the fillers and restarts, check names and numbers, and confirm the tone fits the person you are writing to.

## What messy speech looks like on the page

Say this out loud to a colleague:

> "Um, so tell Sarah I looked at the deck, and the second section, uh, needs another pass, but it's looking good, keep it positive."

Now read the version you would actually send:

> "Hi Sarah, I reviewed the deck. The second section needs another pass, but it's looking good."

The gap between those two lines is the work. Nothing in the first version is wrong. It is shaped for a listener, not a reader. Spoken language repeats itself, pauses, and restarts. Tone of voice carries meaning that a written sentence has to carry in words.

Notice the last clause. "Keep it positive" was a note to yourself, not a line for Sarah. People dictate the brief along with the message all the time. The cleanup pass has to separate the two.

## Why speech comes out messy

People talk faster than they type, and they think while they talk. Ruan et al. measured this for short messages in a 2016 to 2017 study. English speech entry ran at 153 words per minute. Keyboard entry ran at 52 words per minute, a 2.93 times ratio.

Speed is only half the result. The same study counted errors. During entry, speech had fewer corrected errors than the keyboard, 5.30% vs 11.22%. The final speech-entered text kept more uncorrected errors, though, at 1.30% vs 0.79%.

Fewer mistakes happen while you speak. More of the ones that happen survive unless you check.

Be careful with these numbers. The study used touchscreen phones from its era and short messages. It did not measure how long proofreading takes. It cannot tell you how many minutes a cleanup pass will cost on your Mac. Read it as a direction, not a benchmark.

Messy speech also falls into a few recognizable types. Each one needs a different fix:

- **Fillers.** "Um," "uh," "so," "like." Delete them.
- **Restarts.** "Move it to, I mean, push it to next week." Keep only the last version.
- **Vague nouns.** "The thing," "that deck." Swap in the real name.
- **Self-notes.** "Keep it positive," "don't sound like a problem." Let them shape the wording, then remove them.
- **Missing decisions.** "Thursday or Friday." Pick one before you send.

Researchers who study spoken language treat fillers and restarts as their own layer. Zayats and colleagues released a version of the Switchboard corpus with disfluency annotations for careful speech transcripts. Their paper also reports how transcription errors affect the scoring of automatic disfluency detection. MultiTurnCleanup, a 2023 benchmark for spoken conversation, makes a related point. It argues that discontinuities across turns hurt readability.

The practical lesson is simple. A clean transcript keeps the speaker's wording. Finished writing keeps the meaning and drops the scaffolding. For the wider idea, read [From messy thoughts to finished work](/blog/from-messy-thoughts-to-finished-work).

## Transcription and finished writing are two jobs

Most people blur these two jobs together. Splitting them makes the cleanup pass easier to run.

| Job | Input | Output | Who does it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transcription | Your voice | Words in order, fillers included | Dictation engine |
| Cleanup | Rough words | Clean sentences, same meaning | Punctuation commands or a cleanup pass |
| Finishing | Clean draft | Names, numbers and tone checked | You, reading once |

Dictation handles the first job well. Some tools handle part of the second. Only you can do the third, because only you know who is reading and what they need to hear.

This split also explains a common frustration. A tool can produce perfect punctuation and still hand you a message that sounds like a transcript. Correct commas do not make a sentence sound like you.

## How to run the one-pass cleanup

Use this routine for most messages. A short Slack reply takes a minute or two. An email with a real ask takes longer.

1. **Speak the whole thought before you stop.** Do not correct word by word. Every fix while you talk creates another restart.
2. **Keep each pause under 30 seconds.** Apple's Dictation on Mac stops when it hears no speech for 30 seconds. It has no overall time limit, so a short pause does not end your message.
3. **Say punctuation by name when it matters.** "New line" gives one line break. "New paragraph" gives a blank line between paragraphs.
4. **Read the result once for meaning.** Ask whether a stranger would understand the point in one pass.
5. **Delete fillers and restarts.** Cut "um," "so," and any sentence you said twice.
6. **Replace vague nouns with names.** "The thing" becomes the project name. "That deck" becomes the deck's real title.
7. **Check names, numbers and dates against the source.** Dictation misses these most often, so this step matters most.
8. **Remove the self-notes.** Let "keep it positive" shape the wording, then delete the instruction itself.
9. **Read the tone once, as the recipient.** Then send or paste.

Nine steps sound like a lot. In practice, steps 4 and 9 are one read. Steps 5, 6 and 8 happen on the same pass.

## When is the cleanup done?

Stop when the message passes four quick checks. Read it aloud once. If you trip over a clause, split it or cut it.

Next, check that every noun points to something the reader already knows. "The deck" works if they have the deck. "The thing" does not work.

Then ask whether the reader can act on the message without a follow-up question. If they can, you are done.

Reading aloud works because your ear catches what your voice already did. A sentence that felt fine while you spoke can sound clumsy in print. Keep the sentence that sounds like you. Cut the one that sounds like a form letter.

## What the built-in tools support

Three tools cover most Mac writing without extra software. Each one handles cleanup differently.

| Tool | Where it works | Cleanup commands | Limits to know |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apple Dictation on Mac | Any app with a text cursor | "new line," "new paragraph," and punctuation names. Auto-punctuation can be turned off in Dictation settings. | No time limit. Stops after 30 seconds of silence. The on-device cleanup model is English only, on M3 or later Macs with 12GB or more of memory (macOS 27). |
| Word Dictate | Microsoft Word, Home > Dictate. Start with Option+F1 on Mac. | "delete that," "undo," "backspace" plus a number, "new line," "period" | Requires a Microsoft 365 subscription. |
| Google Docs voice typing | Chrome, Edge or Safari. Tools > Voice typing. | "Undo," "Resume," "Stop listening" | Commands work in English only. An administrator can turn voice typing off for an organization. |

Apple's Dictation lets you keep typing on Apple silicon Macs while it listens. Press Escape, the Microphone key, or the Dictation shortcut to stop. That makes it easy to fix one word without losing the rest.

Menu names change between macOS versions and Office releases. If a path above fails, check the Help menu in that app. If your tool is not in the table, run the same nine steps. The commands change, but the routine does not.

## Adjust the cleanup for the message type

The same messy speech needs different cleanup depending on where it lands.

**Slack** wants one or two lines. Drop the greeting if the thread already has one. Put the question first so people can answer quickly.

**Email** wants a greeting, one clear ask, and a sign-off that fits your relationship with the person. Put the date or deadline in the first two sentences. People scan email, and they often miss a deadline buried in paragraph three.

**A document** wants structure. Say "new paragraph" between sections. Turn a list you dictated into short lines, one item per line.

**A code review or ticket** wants the specific thing. "Refactor this to use zustand" survives cleanup better than "make this cleaner." Keep the nouns exact.

For more on email patterns, read [why work emails take so long to write](/for/developers/problems/work-email-writing-takes-hours-weekly). It covers where the time goes.

## Mistakes that make dictated writing sound wrong

- **Fixing while you speak.** Each correction creates a restart. Speak the thought, then fix it.
- **Trusting the first pass for names and numbers.** The Ruan et al. results show uncorrected errors survive in speech-entered text. Check every name and figure.
- **Sending the brief.** People often say the instruction along with the message. Remove it before you send.
- **Keeping the speaking rhythm.** "So, yeah, basically" reads as stalling. Cut the padding, but keep a warm word if it sounds like you.
- **Writing for the wrong reader.** The same sentence can sound fine to a teammate and too blunt to a client. Adjust one line for that reader.
- **Stopping after the first read.** Many leftover errors show up on a second read, after a short break.

## Worked example: from ramble to send

Start with a ramble you might dictate to Slack:

> "hey Mike, um, can we move the, the review to Thursday afternoon, or maybe, I mean, Friday morning? The agenda is the same, but don't make it sound like we messed up the calendar."

Pass one removes fillers and restarts:

> "Hey Mike, can we move the review to Thursday afternoon, or maybe Friday morning? The agenda is the same."

Pass two handles the self-note. "Don't make it sound like we messed up the calendar" steers the wording. It does not belong in the message. The neutral line, "The agenda is the same," already carries the calm tone.

Pass three checks facts and decisions. Mike's name is spelled right. The message still offers two days. You have to choose one before you send, so the cleanup exposed a decision the ramble hid.

Once you pick Thursday, the message asks one question. That is what finished writing looks like in Slack.

## Check where your audio goes

Where your audio goes depends on the tool and its settings. Apple's on-device cleanup for English needs a Mac with an M3 or later chip and at least 12GB of unified memory. Check your chip and memory under Apple menu > About This Mac. On older Macs, or for other languages, that cleanup does not run.

Google voice commands also work only in English. An administrator can turn voice typing off for a company Google account. If you rely on it at work, ask your IT team first.

For more on what stays on the Mac, read [on-device speech to text on Mac](/blog/on-device-speech-to-text-mac).

## Dictating long messages without losing the thread

Long dictations break in one of two places. Either you stop to think and the session ends, or you keep going and the paragraph never closes. Plan for both.

Dictate in short paragraphs. Say "new paragraph" at each break. That keeps the cleanup pass manageable. A long essay becomes several short reads instead of one long one.

If you need to stop, press Escape before you walk away. That ends the session cleanly, and the text you already dictated stays on the page.
