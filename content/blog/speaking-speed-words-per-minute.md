The average speaking speed for English speakers is about 150 words per minute in everyday conversation. For practice presentations, the median is lower, at 111 words per minute, so a talk on stage usually runs slower than a chat with a friend.

That number is a starting point, not a rule. Your pace changes with the audience, the topic, your nerves, and whether you read from a script. This guide covers the main benchmarks, what they mean for presentations, how dictation compares with typing on a Mac, and how to time your own speaking rate with a stopwatch.

## What is the average speaking speed in words per minute?

Speaking rate is measured in words per minute, usually shortened to wpm. You count the words in a passage, then divide by the number of minutes it took to say them. Published figures land between about 100 and 165 wpm, but each one comes from a different situation.

The table below puts the main benchmarks side by side.

| Situation | Words per minute | Where the number comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday conversation, US English | About 150 | National Center for Voice and Speech, as quoted by VirtualSpeech |
| Practice presentations, median | 111 | VirtualSpeech dataset of 32,809 presentations |
| Practice presentations, middle half of speakers | 86 to 133 | Same VirtualSpeech dataset |
| Audience-friendly range for presentations | 100 to 165 | Microsoft Speaker Coach guidance |

The conversation figure is the one most people quote. It is attributed to the National Center for Voice and Speech. I could not find that number on the Center's own website, so treat it as a secondhand estimate, not a measured constant. It is still a useful reference point for how fast people talk when they are not performing.

## Why do speaking speed numbers differ so much?

Different studies count different things. One study may count filler words such as "um" and "like." Another may count only clean sentences from a script. Some measure a full talk. Others measure a single answer. A small change in counting rules can shift the result by a noticeable amount.

Context matters too. A conversation has pauses, interruptions, and short replies such as "yeah" or "right." A prepared talk has fewer pauses and more planned phrases. Those two situations should not share one target.

### Does a faster pace mean a better presentation?

VirtualSpeech split its presentations by score. Talks that scored 8 or higher out of 10 had a median of 138 wpm. Talks that scored below 5 had a median of 81 wpm. That is a correlation in the dataset, not proof that speed causes the score. Strong speakers may simply pace themselves well, and weak talks may drag.

The type of talk also changes the number. Networking conversations had a median of 86 wpm. Elevator pitches had a median of 119 wpm. A quick pitch and a long conference talk should not share the same pace.

Who collected the data matters as well. VirtualSpeech sells presentation practice tools, and its dataset is practice talks. It is not a random sample of everyone who speaks. Use it as a guide to what practice looks like, not as a census of speakers.

## How fast should you speak in a presentation?

Microsoft's Speaker Coach feature for PowerPoint recommends a range of 100 to 165 wpm. Microsoft says this is the rate at which most audiences it tested find information easiest to process. The guidance is based on a field study and past academic research.

Microsoft gives two warnings. Speaking too fast decreases audience comprehension and recall. Speaking too slowly can make the audience lose interest. Microsoft also says people can speak clearly at different rates, so treat the range as a guideline, not a rule.

Here is how the numbers play out with a real script. Suppose your talk script has 500 words:

- At 111 wpm, the VirtualSpeech median, it runs about 4.5 minutes.
- At 150 wpm, the conversation benchmark, it runs about 3.3 minutes.
- At 165 wpm, the top of Microsoft's range, it runs about 3 minutes.

The gap matters when you have a fixed slot. A five-minute lightning talk leaves little room for a rushed delivery or long pauses.

Three mistakes show up again and again.

First, people guess their pace. Time a full read-aloud with a timer instead. Record it, then divide the word count by the minutes.

Second, people speed up when they are nervous. Nerves push pace up without you noticing. Mark pauses in your script at the end of each point, and say those pauses out loud during practice.

Third, people rehearse silently. Silent reading is faster than speaking, so a script that feels fine on your screen can run long on stage. Always rehearse out loud.

A practical fix is to slow your opening. The first minute is when nerves are highest. If you start at a calm pace, the rest of the talk usually settles near your target. Pick two or three points in the script where you want to stop and breathe, then write them in the margin.

## How fast is dictation compared with typing?

The best-known speed comparison for speech input comes from Ruan and colleagues. Their study was first posted as an arXiv preprint. A later journal version is reported with different figures in secondary sources. The numbers below come from the preprint. Check the published paper before you quote them in your own work.

In the English study, 38 people were recruited. Six were discarded, leaving 32 analyzed participants, 16 of them native English speakers. Participants transcribed presented phrases on an iPhone 6 Plus. The speech recognizer ran on a server, not on the phone.

The results were clear. Speech entry ran at 161.20 wpm, including keyboard corrections. The iOS QWERTY keyboard ran at 53.46 wpm. That is a speedup of 3.01 times. Error rates were 2.93% for speech and 3.68% for the keyboard. The preprint gives two different figures for the error reduction, 20.4% in the abstract and 21.6% in the discussion, so pick one with care and cite the source you used.

Now the limits. This was a transcription task. People copied phrases that were already written for them. Writing your own email, report, or notes involves thinking, editing, and pauses. Speech results also depend on the recognizer, the microphone, and how quiet the room is. The study used an older phone, so current hardware and software may behave differently.

Try this sum with the study's rates. A 500-word draft at 53.46 wpm takes about 9.4 minutes on a keyboard. At 161.20 wpm, the same draft takes about 3.1 minutes by voice. Your own numbers will differ, but the direction is the same.

## What are the built-in dictation limits on a Mac?

Apple's Dictation guide for macOS 27 covers the built-in tool. Here is what matters for speed.

Dictation has no maximum length. You can dictate text of any length without a timeout. It stops on its own when no speech is detected for 30 seconds. You can stop it at any time with Escape, the Microphone key, or your Dictation shortcut.

To turn it on, open System Settings > Keyboard, then switch on Dictation. You can start it with the Microphone key, a custom shortcut, Fn pressed twice, or Edit > Start Dictation in the app you are using. Apple's example uses Option-Z, which is ⌥ Z.

Two things trip people up. First, the 30-second rule catches pauses. If you stop to check a fact for half a minute, dictation ends. Press the shortcut again and keep going. Second, Voice Control and standard Dictation do not run together. Apple says standard Dictation is not available when Voice Control is on. If dictation does nothing, check which one is active.

Google Docs has its own voice typing. Open Tools > Voice typing, click the microphone to start, and click it again to stop. Google says it works in the latest Chrome, Edge, and Safari. Punctuation and voice commands are English only. The account and the document language must both be English. Your organization's administrator can turn the feature off. If you write in another language, plan to add punctuation by hand.

## What slows dictation down in real use?

Benchmarks assume a clean run. Real dictation has stops, corrections, and unfamiliar names. Plan for them.

Names and jargon are the usual trouble. A recognizer that hears a new name or a technical term for the first time may guess wrong. Test those words in a scratch note before you dictate a long draft. Say them slowly and clearly the first time, then check how they came out.

Background noise costs more time than most people expect. A fan or a keyboard two desks away can cause a run of small errors. Each error takes a few seconds to fix, and those seconds add up. Sit at a consistent distance from the microphone. Use the same microphone for every test, or your numbers will drift.

Punctuation is where dictation speeds up or stalls. If you pause to decide whether a sentence needs a comma, you lose the rhythm. Speak the full sentence first, then tidy the punctuation in one pass at the end.

Fix errors soon after you speak them. A correction made while the sentence is still fresh takes seconds. A correction made five paragraphs later takes longer, because you have to find the spot again.

## How do you measure your own speaking and dictation speed?

Measure your own pace before you compare yourself with a benchmark. A simple test takes only a few minutes.

1. Pick a passage of about 200 words. Use a news article, a page from a book, or a script you will really speak.
2. Read it aloud at your normal pace. Start the timer on the first word and stop it on the last word.
3. Divide the word count by the minutes. Example: 200 words in 80 seconds is 1.33 minutes, and 200 divided by 1.33 is about 150 wpm.
4. Repeat with a script you would deliver in real life, such as a meeting update.
5. Compare your result with the table earlier in this guide. Do not treat any one number as your final score.

Common mistakes in this test:

- You count words from memory. Use the printed text.
- You rehearse the passage until you know it by heart. Memory speeds you up.
- You stop the timer early. Stop on the last word, not on the end of the next sentence.

Then run the same passage through dictation. Open a blank note, start dictation, and read the passage once. Stop the timer when you finish. Compare that time with your speaking test. If the dictated text needs many fixes, the gap between the two times is your error cost. Note both numbers so you can see where the time goes.

Dictation speed works differently. Your effective dictation speed is your speaking rate minus the time you spend fixing errors. A fast voice that makes many mistakes can end up slower than a steady voice that makes few. ## Mistakes that skew speed numbers

Some errors show up whenever people compare speed figures.

Do not compare a conversation number with a presentation target. The 150 wpm figure describes conversation. The 100 to 165 range describes what audiences find easiest to follow.

Do not use a median as a goal. A median of 111 wpm means the middle speaker in that dataset sat at 111. It is a reference point, not a finish line.

Do not mix counting rules. Count filler words the same way every time, or count none at all. Pick one method and keep it for every test.

Do not treat one lab study as proof for your own work. The Ruan study had 32 analyzed participants and a transcription task. It is useful evidence for speech input in that setting. It is weaker evidence for your Monday email.
