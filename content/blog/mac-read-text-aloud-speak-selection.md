To make your Mac read text out loud, turn on Speak selection in System Settings. Then select the text and press Option-Esc, and press it again to stop.

Setup takes about a minute, and you only do it once. After that, the same shortcut reads any text you select. This guide covers the switch, the shortcut, the voice settings, what happens when nothing is selected, and the problems people hit most often.

## Turn on Speak selection first

Start with the switch. Apple's help page gives this path, and it is the one to follow.

1. Click the Apple menu, then choose System Settings.
2. Click Accessibility in the sidebar.
3. Click Read & Speak.
4. Turn on Speak selection.

Apple now calls the pane Read & Speak. Older guides call it Spoken Content, and search results still show that name. Apple's web address still contains spoken-content, which is why some links look different from the menu you see.

If the names on your screen do not match this page, match them by order. The path stays the same, even when a label changes. The version note near the end of this guide explains why.

## Read selected text with Option-Esc

Select the text you want. Drag across a paragraph, or click at the start and Shift-click at the end. Then press Option-Esc, which appears as ⌥ Esc in many menus. Your Mac starts reading from the selection.

Press ⌥ Esc again to stop. Apple's help page says that by default, the Mac speaks text when you press Option-Esc. The same key stops it. You do not need to open a menu or a window.

Good candidates include an email someone sent you, a long article, and a draft you wrote. Clean paragraphs read best. Tables, lists, and code blocks are harder to follow by ear, so select one section at a time.

## What happens when you select nothing

Press ⌥ Esc without selecting anything, and your Mac reads the text it can find in the current window. Apple's page says this directly. If there is no text to speak, your Mac plays a sound instead.

That short sound causes most of the confusion. You press the shortcut on a page, hear a chime, and assume the feature is broken. Usually the window has no readable text where you expected it. Click into the paragraph, select it, and press ⌥ Esc again.

A simple rule helps. Select when you want one specific part. Leave the selection empty when you want the whole window read, such as a page with one main block of text.

## Mistakes people make with selection

Most selection problems come from selecting too much or too little. Three habits cause the most trouble.

The first is selecting a heading and the body under it in one drag. The voice reads the heading, pauses, and then reads the body, which sounds fine. The trouble starts when the heading is a navigation bar or a menu label. You hear "Home, About, Pricing, Blog" before the article starts. Start your selection at the first sentence of the article instead.

The second is dragging across two columns. On a page with a sidebar, a drag can pull in text from both sides, and the voice reads one line from the left and one from the right. Select one column at a time when a page uses columns.

The third is expecting a PDF to behave like a web page. The feature needs text it can select. If you cannot highlight the words with your cursor, the feature has nothing to read. Test a short passage first. If it stays silent, the file may be a scan, and you need a text version of the document.

## Change the shortcut and the reading options

Option-Esc is the default, but it can clash with a shortcut you already use. To change it:

1. Open Apple menu > System Settings > Accessibility > Read & Speak.
2. Click the Info button next to Speak selection.
3. Press the key combination you want.

Apple's example is Option+Tab. Pick something you can reach with one hand, and check that no other app already uses it. A combination with Command in it is less likely to clash with typing, but it is also harder to reach while you hold a cup or a trackpad.

The same Info panel holds more settings. Keyboard shortcut is the one you just changed. Highlight content marks words as they are spoken, which helps when you need to follow along. Word color and Sentence color set the highlight colors. Sentence style controls how the current sentence is marked. Show controller displays the speech controller on screen while text is read.

Turn on Highlight content for dense material, such as a contract or a research paper. It keeps your place. Turn it off for quick listening, since the highlight is then just extra movement on screen.

## Pick a voice and set the speed

ElevenLabs' Mac guide, a vendor blog, says the speaking rate can be adjusted with a slider. It also says macOS offers a limited selection of voices, which vary in gender, accent, and language. Apple's help page does not spell out these controls, so open the Read & Speak pane and look for the voice and rate options.

Some practical choices:

- Use a faster rate for material you already know.
- Slow down for technical text, legal wording, or anything with names and numbers.
- Try two or three voices on the same paragraph before you commit. Listen for a minute, not a sentence.

A voice you find tiring will make you stop using the feature, so choose one you can listen to for twenty minutes.

## Worked example: a long article in Safari

Say you want to hear a long article you found in Safari. Start by scrolling to the first sentence of the body text, not the headline. Click at the start of that sentence and Shift-click at the end of the section you want. Press ⌥ Esc, and listen.

When the first section ends, press ⌥ Esc to stop. Scroll down, select the next section, and press the shortcut again. Apple's help page does not describe how the feature moves through a very long selection, so test a short section first. Working in chunks keeps the highlights accurate and makes it easy to stop and resume.

This approach also helps when you return to the article later. You know exactly where you stopped, because you selected the section by hand.

## Common problems and how to fix them

Most problems come down to four causes. The table lists the symptom, the likely cause, and the fix.

| What you see | Likely cause | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| A short sound, no speech | No readable text in the window | Select the text, then press ⌥ Esc |
| Nothing happens at all | Speak selection is off, or another shortcut uses the same keys | Turn on the switch in Read & Speak, then change the shortcut in the Info panel |
| Speech keeps going | You have not pressed the shortcut again | Press ⌥ Esc to stop |
| It reads more than you wanted | Your selection was wider than planned | Select a smaller range and press ⌥ Esc |
| You cannot find the feature | You are looking in Keyboard or Sound settings | Open Accessibility > Read & Speak |

The most common mistake is pressing the shortcut before selecting anything. The second most common is forgetting that the same key stops playback. If you change the shortcut and forget it, check the Info panel before you search the web for a fix.

## Speak selection is not VoiceOver

People mix these two up. Speak selection reads text you choose, when you press a shortcut. VoiceOver is Apple's separate screen reader, and it works differently. It moves through the interface, announces controls, and is built for people who navigate without seeing the screen.

If you want to hear one article, Speak selection is the simpler tool. If you need to operate your whole Mac by ear, look into VoiceOver in the same Accessibility area. Do not expect one to replace the other.

## Why read-aloud helps some readers

Read-aloud is not only for people with a diagnosed condition. Many adults find long text slow going, and listening gives them another way in. NCES reports that in 2023, 28% of U.S. adults performed at literacy Level 1 or below, up from 19% in 2017. That is more than one in four adults, which is a good reason to keep the feature easy to reach.

Research on the tool itself is narrower. A 2018 meta-analysis in the Journal of Learning Disabilities looked at text-to-speech for students with reading disabilities. The pooled effect on reading comprehension was 0.35, with a 95% confidence interval of 0.14 to 0.56. That study covers students, not adults, and an effect size is not a promise for you. Read it as a sign that read-aloud can help, then test it on your own reading.

## Use read-aloud to proofread your drafts

Hearing a draft catches problems your eyes skip. Repeated words, missing words, and wrong names stand out when you hear them.

Try this with an email before you send it. Select the body, press ⌥ Esc, and listen with the screen turned away or your eyes closed. Stop when you hear something wrong, fix it, and select the paragraph again.

A slower rate helps for proofreading. A faster rate suits articles you already know. Keep the same voice while you proofread, so odd word choices stand out against a familiar sound.

## A routine that fits a normal day

Say you have a long newsletter and a full inbox. Select the newsletter, press ⌥ Esc, and let it run while you sort your email. You are not trying to absorb every word. You are keeping your hands free for the task in front of you.

Keep one shortcut for this habit. Changing keys every week makes the feature harder to remember. If you need a different setup for a single app, change the shortcut once and leave it alone.

## Check which macOS version you run

Open Apple menu > About This Mac to see your version. Apple's Read & Speak help page has a version selector that goes up to macOS 27, but the page does not say which release its steps describe. I could not confirm the exact steps for each version, so treat the names in this guide as the current Apple labels.

If your menu uses Spoken Content, keep the same order: Accessibility, then the read-aloud pane, then the Speak selection switch. The labels change over time. The place you look does not change much.

Work through the setup once, test it on a paragraph you know, and you will have the feature working in under a minute.
