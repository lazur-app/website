Context switching is what happens when you leave the window you're working in to get a small answer somewhere else. To stop it, get the answer where you already are, using help that can see your screen or the search and help built into the app you're using.

This guide covers what the switching costs, where it happens on a Mac, and how to get help in the apps you already use. It ends with a simple way to measure your own switches.

## What does context switching cost you at work?

A context switch feels cheap. You open a chat tab, check one thing, and go back. The seconds in the tab are not the real cost. The real cost is rebuilding your place afterward. You have to remember which sentence you were writing, which line you were reading, and what you meant to try next.

Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index puts the problem in numbers. It says employees who use Microsoft 365 get interrupted every 2 minutes by a meeting, email, or notification. The same report gives a count of 275 interruptions a day. It describes that window two ways, as core work hours in one place and as a 24-hour day in another. Treat 275 as a rough signal, not a precise measure.

The survey found that 48% of employees say their work feels chaotic and fragmented. Among leaders, the share is 52%. It covered 31,000 full-time employed or self-employed knowledge workers across 31 markets, plus an extra US sample, between February and March 2025.

Asana's Anatomy of Work research makes a related point. It says the average person spends 60% of their day on work about work. That means status checks, searching, and chasing answers, rather than the work itself. The Asana page does not state the survey year, so read that figure as a direction, not a benchmark.

Be careful with one number you'll see everywhere. Many articles say it takes 23 minutes to get back on track after an interruption. A 2025 article from BigGo Finance traces that figure to a 2006 interview with researcher Gloria Mark, not to a published study. Don't quote it as settled fact. The point holds without it. Every switch costs you something, and that cost is bigger than the seconds you spent away.

## Where does context switching happen on a Mac?

Most switches are small, and they come from the same few situations. The table below lists the usual ones, the detour most people take, and a shorter path.

| Small question | The usual detour | A shorter path |
|---|---|---|
| “What does this error mean?” | Copy the text, open a chat tab, paste, read, switch back | Ask about the error on your screen |
| “Where is this setting?” | Search the web, open a help article, hunt through menus | Use the app's own search, like Help > Search the menus in Google Docs |
| “Remind me to send the invoice” | Open Reminders, type the task, set a date, close the app | Say the reminder out loud and keep typing |
| “Reply to this message” | Switch to a text editor, draft, copy, paste back | Dictate the reply at your cursor |
| “When is the deadline in this thread?” | Open email, search for the thread, scan for the date | Ask about the open thread or search it in place |

Read the table as a pattern. Each shorter path keeps your eyes on the window you started in. The best option is not always an AI tool. Sometimes it's an in-app search you forgot existed.

## How do you get help inside Google Docs, Figma, and Word?

Start with the apps you open every day. Several big tools now include help that stays inside the app. The quality varies, so check each one before you rely on it.

**Google Docs.** The fastest route is the built-in search. Click Search in the toolbar, or go to Help > Search the menus. On a Mac, the shortcut is ⌥ /. Type what you want, such as “line spacing” or “comments,” and Docs finds the tool for you. You never leave the document.

**Figma.** Figma routes help through a question mark icon in the bottom-right corner of any page. Click it, then choose Contact support. That opens Fig, Figma's AI support chatbot. Fig is available on all plans, but it only works in English.

Escalation depends on your plan. Starter and Education users go to the community forum for direct help. Professional, Organization, and Enterprise users go to email support. Figma does not offer phone or live chat support. If you want a written guide instead, the Figma Help Center at help.figma.com has articles. The support page does not describe an in-app path to that center, so open it in a browser.

**Microsoft Word.** In Word for Microsoft 365 for Mac, the Copilot button sits in the lower-right corner of the document by default. You can move it to sit beside the ribbon. Copilot needs a Copilot license. If the button is missing, your subscription or organization may not include Copilot. If you're on Semi-Annual Enterprise Channel, Copilot isn't available there, so you need to move to Current Channel or Monthly Enterprise Channel. If your account is managed, IT controls the privacy settings. Microsoft 365 Family and Personal subscribers can turn Copilot off.

Word's Copilot FAQ includes a Get help with Copilot section with three suggested prompts. Those prompts open a Copilot chat. That is still a detour, just a smaller one.

The pattern is simple. Good in-app help answers inside the document. Weaker help sends you to a support queue or a separate chat. When you set up a new app, find its help path first, before you need it.

## How do you ask for help without leaving the app?

Asking well matters more when the answer comes back fast. A vague question gets a vague answer. Use these habits.

- **Point at the thing.** Ask “what does this button do?” instead of “how do I do this in the app?” The first question matches the screen you see.
- **Ask where first, then how.** “Where is line spacing?” gets a location. “Walk me through it” gets the steps. Splitting them keeps you from skipping ahead.
- **Keep the exact words.** Copy error messages word for word. A summary like “it broke” sends the answer somewhere vague.
- **Ask one thing at a time.** Two questions in one request produce two answers you have to sort out.

The common mistake is describing the screen from memory. If the assistant can see the window, let it look. If it can't, paste the relevant text, not your paraphrase of it.

The second mistake is asking, then checking the answer in a browser anyway. For low-stakes settings, trust the first answer and try it. For money, legal, medical, or security settings, confirm with the vendor's own documentation. That check is worth the switch.

## How do you keep small tasks out of your head?

Small tasks cause the most switching. They are not hard. They just pull you out of the work, even briefly. You remember you need to do something, you start to open the app, and the thread is gone by the time the app loads.

The fix is to capture the task the moment it appears, without opening anything. A voice command or quick dictation does that. Get the task out of your head, then return to the work.

Three examples from a normal day:

- You're drafting a proposal. A colleague says the deadline moved to later in the week. You set a reminder to confirm the new date with the client on Friday morning, then keep writing.
- You kick off a slow test run. You set a timer for the expected finish, then keep reading the code you meant to review.
- You need to answer a message, but you're mid-sentence. You hold the dictation key, say the reply, and let the text land at the cursor. Read it once before you send.

If a task takes more than a sentence, park it for later. Batch those into one planned moment, such as the end of a block. Your in-the-moment job is to get the task out of your head, not to finish it.

## What are the mistakes that bring context switching back?

Some habits quietly undo everything above. Watch for these four.

1. **Checking notifications on instinct.** Every banner is a switch you didn't choose. Turn off banners for apps that don't need you, and use a Focus mode for deep work. The settings are in [how to stop Mac notifications from distracting you](/for/developers/problems/mac-notifications-distracting-deep-work).
2. **Batching questions to save time.** It feels efficient. By the time you come back, you have forgotten half of them, and the afternoon fills with catch-up.
3. **Sending long research into your focus block.** Long research belongs in the background. Read the result at a planned moment, not in the middle of a function you're writing.
4. **Using a general chatbot for everything.** A chat tab with no context means you copy the whole problem every time. Ask from the window where the problem lives, or check the app's own help first.

## How do you measure your own context switching?

You can't fix what you don't count. For one working week, keep a tally. Each time you leave your main app for another window or tab, make a mark. Next to the mark, write a few words about why you left.

At the end of the week, sort the reasons into three groups:

- Answers you could have found in place, such as a setting, a menu, or an error message.
- Real research that needed a browser or another source.
- Breaks you chose on purpose.

The first group is your target. Those are the switches you can cut. The second group is real work, so leave it alone. The third group is healthy.

Pick the top three from the first group and find a shorter path for each one. The table earlier is a starting point. Then run the tally again the following week. If the marks go down and the reasons change, the habit is working.

Don't set a target from the Microsoft figure. A rough industry number tells you little about your own afternoon. Your tally shows exactly where your switches go.
