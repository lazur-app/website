In 2015, about 1.68% of US workers had carpal tunnel syndrome (CTS) that a clinician linked to their job. Depending on how a study defines a case, published rates run from under 3% to more than 30%, so the number you quote depends on the question you ask.

## What the 2015 federal survey says about work related CTS

The number people quote most often comes from the 2015 National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) Occupational Health Supplement. The CDC and NIOSH publish the results as a chart. The chart uses an estimated base of 145.7 million workers, and it gives three numbers that matter here.

| Measure, 2015 NHIS | Share of workers | Estimated workers |
|---|---|---|
| Ever diagnosed with CTS by a clinician | 6.05% | About 8.82 million |
| Current CTS | 2.67% | About 3.89 million |
| Current CTS attributed to work | 1.68% | About 2.45 million |

The bottom row answers the narrow question. How many workers had CTS when they were surveyed, and did a clinician link it to work? The top row is broader. It counts anyone ever told they had CTS, and it does not filter for work.

Check the math yourself. 2.67% of 145.7 million is about 3.89 million, which matches the chart. Do the same check on any percentage you quote. It takes ten seconds and catches copied errors.

Two details matter. First, the 95% confidence interval for the work-attributed row runs from 1.46% to 1.92%. Survey estimates always carry a margin like that, so treat 1.68% as a best estimate, not an exact count. Second, the chart does not define "ever" or "current." It also does not explain how a case was judged work related.

Survey data depend on what people report and what a clinician told them. A worker with wrist symptoms who never saw a doctor does not show up in these counts. So 1.68% measures the narrow case. It is not a full count of every wrist problem at work.

## Why clinical studies report higher rates

Surveys ask people. Clinical studies test them. Much of the gap between published numbers comes from how each study defines a case.

A pooled analysis of six US prospective studies of workers looked at this directly. Prospective studies follow people forward over time. This analysis enrolled 4,321 workers from more than 50 workplaces, with data collected from 2001 to 2010. The authors reported three rates, depending on the rule they used.

| Case definition in the pooled analysis | Share of workers |
|---|---|
| Electrodiagnostic confirmation (the strictest rule) | 7.8% (338 cases) |
| Symptoms alone | 15.2% |
| Abnormal nerve test alone | 31.0% |

Each row answers a different question. Symptoms alone asks whether a worker hurts or tingles. An abnormal nerve test alone flags a test result, whether or not the worker has symptoms. The 7.8% row asks for a confirmed case, so it is the strictest of the three.

Pick the row that fits your question. If you want to know how many workers feel CTS symptoms, the 15.2% row is closer. If you want confirmed cases for a safety review, the 7.8% row is the better fit.

A study outside the US shows the same pattern. A 1999 JAMA study in Scania, Sweden, surveyed 3,000 adults aged 25 to 74, and 2,466 responded. It found median neuropathy at the wrist in 4.9% of them. Only 2.7% were confirmed both clinically and electrophysiologically. Symptoms in the median nerve area appeared in 14.4%. That was a general population study, not a worker study, so keep it apart from the US worker figures.

## How to read a workplace CTS percentage

Headlines rarely name the definition. Run through four questions before you repeat a figure.

1. What year was the data collected? The 2015 survey and the 2001 to 2010 pooled study are different datasets.
2. What counts as a case? Symptoms, a clinician's diagnosis, an abnormal nerve test, and a confirmed case all give different numbers.
3. What is the base? 1.68% is a share of all workers. 67.1% is a share of current cases. Those are different denominators.
4. Is there a margin? Look for a confidence interval. If none is given, ask where the number came from.

Here is a worked example. Say a blog post claims that 15% of workers have carpal tunnel. The 15.2% row for symptoms alone in the pooled study is close to that figure. The same study puts confirmed cases at 7.8%. A careful writer would say the 15% figure describes symptoms, then check which one the post meant.

## What the 2010 survey adds, and why it is not a trend

The earlier federal survey, the 2010 NHIS, covered 27,157 adults. Of them, 17,524 were current or recent workers. Among those workers, 6.7% had ever been diagnosed with CTS by a clinician. The 12-month figure was 3.1%, about 4.8 million workers. Clinicians attributed 67.1% of current cases to work. In that survey, the attribution was self-reported.

It is tempting to place the 2010 numbers next to the 2015 numbers. Don't. The surveys differ in year, and their definitions may differ too. They also use different bases. The 67.1% figure is a share of cases. The 1.68% figure is a share of all workers.

Put them together and you get a line like "two-thirds of CTS is work related." That line holds only for the 2010 current cases. It is not a rate for all workers, and it is not a trend from 2010 to 2015.

## Who has the highest rates at work

The pooled analysis also split its numbers by sex and age. CTS prevalence was 10.0% in women and 5.8% in men. It was 11.9% in workers aged 50 and over, compared with 3.7% in workers under 30.

Those are two separate splits. A 55-year-old woman sits in both higher-rate groups. The study does not report a rate for that exact combination, so do not multiply the two numbers together. The study reports the gap, not the cause.

If you are 50 or older, the 11.9% figure is the one that applies to you. Watch for early symptoms and mention them to your clinician sooner rather than later.

Job type matters too. A trade article in Occupational Health & Safety puts general population CTS at 1 to 5%, and 5 to 21% in manufacturing and meatpacking. That article cites other sources instead of running a new survey. Use its ranges as context, not as a precise measurement.

## How many new cases appear each year

Prevalence is a snapshot. It counts people who have the condition at one point in time. Incidence counts new cases over a period. If you want to know how fast cases appear, incidence is the number to read.

The pooled analysis reported 2.3 new cases per 100 person-years (95% CI 2.0 to 2.7). That came from 204 new cases over 8,833 person-years. One person-year is one worker observed for one year. So 100 workers observed for a year make 100 person-years.

Here is a worked example. If 1,000 workers were followed for one year at that rate, you would expect about 23 new cases. That is a scale check, not a forecast for your workplace.

Passive surveillance gives a much lower number. Workers' compensation data reported 0.04 to 0.2 new cases per 100 person-years. The research rate is more than ten times the top of that range. The gap has a simple cause. Workers' compensation counts people who file a claim. Mild cases often never reach that stage, so the official count runs low.

## What raises the risk at work

The strongest work-based evidence comes from a 2022 systematic review and meta-analysis. It included 17 prospective cohort studies published between 2001 and 2021. Twelve were pooled. Together they covered 1,051,707 workers and 9,270 clinically assessed CTS cases.

The review reports hazard ratios (HR). An HR compares how fast cases appear in an exposed group with how fast they appear in an unexposed group. An HR of 1.0 means no difference. Here are the three exposures with the clearest signal.

- High repetition: HR 1.87 (95% CI 1.42 to 2.46), high certainty.
- High force intensity: HR 1.84 (95% CI 1.22 to 2.79), high certainty.
- Hand activity above the ACGIH action level: HR 1.75 (95% CI 1.40 to 2.17), four studies, high certainty.

Force duration showed an HR of 1.80, but its interval runs from 0.98 to 3.31. The certainty rating was very low, so treat that result as unsettled.

The most common mistake is reading HR 1.87 as "87% of cases come from repetition." The review does not say that. It says people with high repetition had about 1.87 times the rate of people without it, in these studies. A rate ratio describes risk. It does not split cases into causes.

The review also has a scope limit. Its studies were mostly cohort studies, and some came from outside the US. It does not measure how common work related CTS is across US workplaces. It tells you which work conditions go with higher rates.

## Edge cases that change which number applies

The same person can land in different rows. Take a warehouse worker who has night tingling in several fingers and has never seen a clinician. That person counts toward the symptoms-alone row in a study that asks about symptoms. They do not count in the NHIS rows, which need a clinician's diagnosis. They also do not count in the confirmed row, because nobody tested their nerves.

Another case: a worker diagnosed years ago who now has a different job. The "ever" row counts them. The "current" row counts them only if the survey treats their symptoms as current, and the chart does not define that.

A third case is mixed work. Someone who types part of the day and lifts the rest has two sets of exposures. The chart does not say how the survey weighed mixed tasks. Attribution goes to a clinician, so describe every task you do, not just the one that hurts most. The 2010 survey took attribution from self-reports, which means your description can shape the count.

## Common mistakes when quoting these numbers

- **Quoting 1.68% as the total.** That is the narrow, survey-based, work-attributed count. Say which measure you mean.
- **Stitching 2010 and 2015 into a trend.** The years, definitions and bases differ.
- **Treating symptoms as a diagnosis.** Symptoms alone reads 15.2%. Confirmed cases read 7.8% in the same study.
- **Using workers' compensation counts as the true rate.** They sit at 0.04 to 0.2 per 100 person-years. The pooled study reports 2.3.
- **Turning a hazard ratio into a share of cases.** HR 1.87 is a rate ratio, not a percentage of cases.
- **Citing the 1 to 5% general population range as a survey result.** It comes from a trade article that cites earlier sources.

## What to do if this sounds like your wrist

1. Write down the symptoms. Note which fingers tingle or go numb, whether they wake you at night, and how long they have lasted.
2. Log your tasks for one week. Count hours of typing, mouse use, gripping tools and repeated motions. The review tied high repetition and high force to CTS, so those are the two loads to watch.
3. See a clinician soon. Say what your work involves, and ask whether a nerve test fits your case. Nerve testing is how the confirmed-case rates in this guide were built.
4. Tell your employer or safety officer in writing, and ask for a workstation review. Keep a copy of what you sent.
5. Change the load where you can. Take breaks from repetitive work, ease off hard grips, and ask your clinician which changes suit you.
