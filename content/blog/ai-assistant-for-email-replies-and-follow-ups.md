An **AI assistant for email replies** reads the thread you have open, takes a short instruction, and drafts a message you can edit before you send it. It handles the routine work well: scheduling, follow-ups, thank-you notes, and tone fixes. Every draft still needs your check for facts and commitments.

This guide covers what these tools do, how to ask for the reply you want, which built-in options exist in Gmail and Outlook for Mac, and what to check before you send. It also covers the part many guides skip: how recipients react when they suspect a machine wrote the message.

## What an AI assistant for email replies actually does

Most email assistants do three jobs. They read the thread for context. They turn your instruction into a draft. Then they adjust that draft for length, tone, or clarity.

Context matters most. A reply to “Can we move the call?” reads differently when the thread shows the call has already moved twice. A good assistant reads the earlier messages, the names, and the open question. A weak one writes a generic reply that could go to anyone.

Here is a simple test. Could the draft go to a different person about a different topic without a word changing? If yes, the assistant did not really use the thread. Ask again and point it at a specific detail, such as the budget decision made on Monday.

Speed is the reason people reach for these tools. Speed alone is not enough. A fast draft that misses the question still costs you a second round of email.

## Reply, follow up, or summarize: pick the right task

Different emails need different instructions. Use this table to match the task to the request you give.

| Task | What you give the assistant | What to check before sending |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reply to a question | The answer in plain words, plus the tone | Facts, dates, and any figure you quote |
| Follow up on silence | The original ask, the deadline, and how firm to sound | Whether the earlier message actually asked for something |
| Change the tone | The current draft and the target, such as warmer but not apologetic | Whether the new tone hides a real problem |
| Summarize a long thread | Who the summary is for and what decision they need | Names, decisions, and who owns the next step |
| Decline politely | The reason, any alternative, and how close the relationship is | Promises you did not mean to make |

Follow-ups deserve their own approach. A follow-up is a second message on purpose. It should be shorter than the first, and it should make the next step easy to answer.

Summaries are underrated. If a thread runs long, ask for the decision and the open item before you ask for a reply. You then answer the current state of the conversation, not just the last message. That habit prevents a lot of replies that repeat what someone already knows.

## How to write a request that gets a usable draft

A strong request has a few parts. Use this order when you speak or type.

- **Intent:** reply, follow up, clarify, decline, or thank.
- **Facts:** dates, decisions, status, or the next step you need.
- **Tone:** warm, direct, concise, formal, or casual.
- **Limits:** length, what not to promise, and who else should stay on the thread.

You do not need to repeat the subject line or the recipient’s name if the assistant can read the open thread. Repeating them wastes time. It can also cause errors if the names in your request differ from the names in the thread.

Compare two requests for the same email.

- Weak: “Reply to this.”
- Strong: “Say Thursday afternoon works for the call, ask them to send the deck before then, keep it short, and do not commit to a budget.”

The second request gives the assistant clear decisions to respect. It does not have to guess. It also tells you what to check afterward. Did the draft mention the deck? Did it stay short? Did it avoid the budget?

Speaking helps here. Most people say more context out loud than they type, and that extra context often improves the draft. If you want to compare your own speaking pace with typing, the [speaking versus typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test) gives you a quick reading.

## Gmail, Outlook, and the AI already in your mail app

You may not need a separate tool at all. Both Gmail and Outlook for Mac now include AI drafting. The details differ, and the details decide what you can use.

### Gmail

Google’s announcement on January 8, 2026 says Help me write is available to everyone in the US from that day. It also says Suggested Replies, an update to Smart Replies that uses conversation context and your writing style, is rolling out to everyone at no cost. Proofread, which checks grammar, tone, and style before you send, is limited to Google AI Pro and Ultra subscribers. The [Google announcement](https://blog.google/products-and-platforms/products/gmail/gmail-is-entering-the-gemini-era/) lays out these details.

Eligibility is the catch. Google’s Gmail help page says Help me write needs an eligible Google Workspace or Google AI plan, and that free personal accounts can use it only in the US. The two pages do not fully agree. Check the [Gmail help page](https://support.google.com/mail/answer/13955415?hl=en) against your own account before you plan a workflow around it. If you use Gmail on Mac every day, the [Gmail assistant page](/apps/gmail) covers the voice side of replying in the same window.

The help page describes Gemini drafting and refining in the compose window. It does not say Gemini sends mail on its own. Read it as a drafting aid, not a sender.

### Outlook for Mac

Microsoft’s support page describes Reply with Copilot. You select the message, choose the Copilot icon, and pick Summarize and reply to this email. You can then adjust the draft for tone or length, or regenerate it. The [Microsoft Support page](https://support.microsoft.com/en-us/office/create-quicker-responses-using-reply-with-copilot-in-outlook-for-mac-795e9819-b714-4d05-9a41-75c1c3aebfd0) says this works only in the latest version of Outlook for Mac, and that legacy Outlook is not supported.

The same page does not list a license requirement. It also does not say whether Copilot can send a message. It only describes drafts. Microsoft’s [Copilot for work page](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/microsoft-365-copilot/copilot-for-work) says organizations need eligible Microsoft 365 or Office 365 licenses, and it lists Copilot Chat helping compose emails in Outlook. That page shows no prices. Ask your IT admin what your organization has turned on.

### Built-in tools or a voice assistant

Built-in drafting lives inside one app. A voice assistant can help with a thread in several apps, including documents you open next to your mail. Both can be useful. The better question is where you do most of your replies. If nearly all of your email lives in Gmail, start with the built-in tool and see whether it covers your routine replies. If your work jumps between mail, chat, and documents, a tool that sits across those apps may save more steps.

## Writing a follow-up that gets an answer

A follow-up fails for one of two reasons. It is too vague to answer, or it sounds like an accusation. The fix is the same for both. Name the thing, name the date, and make the next step small.

This pattern works for most work follow-ups:

- Open with the original ask in one line.
- Give the new date or deadline.
- Offer one clear next step, such as a yes or no, or a short call if that is easier for them.
- Close without apologizing for the follow-up.

Here is a request you can say out loud: “Follow up on the pricing question from Tuesday, ask whether they can confirm by Friday noon, offer a short call if that is easier, and keep the tone friendly.”

Wait long enough that the first message had a fair chance to be read. A follow-up sent an hour later reads as impatience. Use your own judgment about how long that is for each relationship.

After the draft appears, read only the verbs. Look for “I will,” “we can,” and “by tomorrow.” Those words make promises. Delete any promise you have not agreed to keep.

## Common mistakes people make with AI email drafts

The first mistake is letting the draft make a promise. AI drafts often say “I will send that today” or “we can do the full scope.” Read every verb that commits you to a date, a price, or a deliverable.

The second mistake is trusting names and dates. A draft can copy the wrong name from the thread, or pick the wrong Thursday. Check each name and date against the source message. This takes less time than explaining an error to a client.

The third mistake is sending the first draft. A first draft is a starting point. If it sounds like a brochure, ask for something plainer: “Cut the opener, keep the question, and remove any sentence that sounds like a press release.”

The fourth mistake is over-polishing a sensitive email. An apology, a conflict, or bad news needs your own voice. Use the assistant to clear clutter, then rewrite the one or two sentences that carry the feeling.

The fifth mistake is forgetting the attachment. Drafts sometimes say “attached” when nothing is attached. Check the attachment list before you press send.

The sixth mistake is asking for three jobs at once. “Summarize the thread, reply to the client, and book a call” is a chain of separate tasks. Run them one at a time so you can check each result before the next one starts.

## Should you tell recipients AI helped?

This question matters more than the speed of a draft. The research gives a useful picture.

In a 2026 Omni Calculator survey of 705 employed US adults who use AI for writing, 76% say they rarely or never tell recipients that AI reviewed or helped write what they sent. Only 7% disclose routinely. The same survey found 77% of AI-using workers check AI feedback for grammar and clarity. The [Omni Calculator report](https://www.omnicalculator.com/reports/ai-second-opinion-survey) has the full breakdown.

Recipients have opinions too. In a separate Omni Calculator survey of 921 US workers, reported by Forbes, 30% of recipients say AI-assisted email signals the sender did not care enough. 27% say they trust the message less. Also, 51% prefer an imperfect email a coworker wrote themselves. 25% prefer AI-generated content, and 25% have no preference. The [Forbes article](https://www.forbes.com/sites/michelletravis/2026/08/26/ai-is-transforming-work-emails-and-not-all-humans-like-the-result/) reports these figures.

These numbers describe perception. They do not measure whether AI-assisted replies get better or worse results. Still, they point to a practical rule. Use AI to remove clutter and check facts. Keep the feeling, the apology, and the decision in your own words.

Disclosure is a judgment call. A routine scheduling reply rarely needs a note. A hard conversation with a client needs your own phrasing more than it needs speed.

## Why email volume makes this worth the effort

Email volume is the reason to care about any of this. Workplace Insight, reporting on Microsoft’s 2025 Work Trend Index, says the average employee receives 117 emails a day. The same coverage says almost a third of active users have returned to their inboxes by 10 p.m. The [Workplace Insight article](https://workplaceinsight.net/microsoft-research-lays-bare-the-rise-of-the-infinite-workday/) has the details.

Look for patterns in your own replies. Pull up your last few sent messages and note what you rewrote most. Common problems are length, stiffness, and a missing detail. An assistant can help with all three, if you tell it which problem you are fixing.

Measure one week. Count how many replies needed a second draft, and what the second draft fixed. That gives you a clear view of where a tool helps and where it adds a step.

## Privacy and what to check before you use any assistant

Email holds names, money, health details, and contracts. Before you use any assistant on it, check three things.

First, find out where the content goes. Ask whether the assistant sends the thread to a cloud model, and whether it keeps a copy. Read the privacy policy, not only the marketing page.

Second, check what the tool can see. A tool that reads only the active window is easier to reason about than one that reads your whole inbox. Some tools need screen recording permission on macOS. Grant it only after you understand what gets captured.

Third, check what you paste. Remove account numbers, passwords, and medical details from the thread before you paste it into a chat window, unless your organization has approved that tool. Your organization’s policy beats any habit you have built.
