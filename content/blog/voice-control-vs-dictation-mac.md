On a Mac, Voice Control and standard Dictation cannot run at the same time. When Voice Control is on, it handles your dictation, and standard Dictation is unavailable until you turn Voice Control off.

Apple states this plainly in the Mac Help guides for both features. This page explains what each tool does, how to switch between them, and which one fits the work you do.

## Why Voice Control and Dictation cannot run together

Apple's guide to Voice Control commands says: "When Voice Control is on, you use Voice Control to dictate text; standard macOS Dictation isn't available." The Dictation guide repeats the rule in its own words.

Apple does not say why the two features conflict. The pages I checked describe the behavior and stop there. So this page describes what you see on your screen, not the engineering behind it.

The guides I checked were written for macOS 27. I did not check the guides for Tahoe 26 or Sequoia 15, so their wording may differ. Some older Apple pages still say System Preferences where current ones say System Settings. If your menu names do not match this page, check the Mac Help for your release first.

In practice, the rule is simple. Turn Voice Control on, and standard Dictation goes away. Turn Voice Control off, and it comes back. You cannot run both at once and switch between them in the middle of a sentence.

## Voice Control vs Dictation on Mac: the differences

Both tools turn speech into text. After that, they part ways. Standard Dictation types what you say. Voice Control types what you say, and it also runs commands such as "Open Mail" or "Click Done."

The table uses the settings and limits from Apple's guides. Where Apple says nothing, the table says so.

| | Standard Dictation | Voice Control |
|---|---|---|
| Where to turn it on | System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation | System Settings > Accessibility > Voice Control |
| What it does | Turns speech into text | Turns speech into text and runs Mac commands |
| Modes | None listed in Apple's Dictation guide | Dictation, Spelling, Command, and Swift |
| Silence limit | Stops after 30 seconds with no speech | Not stated in the pages I checked |
| Length limit | None, according to Apple | Not stated in the pages I checked |
| Internet | Not stated in the pages I checked | Needed once for a one-time download, then not needed |
| Best for | Typing text by voice | Running the Mac by voice, including text |

The single phrase shows the gap best. Say "Open Mail" to Voice Control, and Mail opens. Say it to standard Dictation, and the words land in your text field. Most of the choice between the two comes down to that difference.

## How standard Dictation works on Mac

Dictation is the simpler tool. It turns speech into text at your cursor. It does not open apps or press buttons.

Turn it on in System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation. In the same pane, you can change the key combination that starts and stops it. The guide I checked does not name a default shortcut. Check the setting on your own Mac before you rely on a key press.

Two limits matter in daily use. Dictation stops by itself after 30 seconds with no speech. It has no length limit, and Apple says so directly: "You can dictate text of any length without a timeout." A long draft is fine. A long pause is not.

Here is how that plays out. You are dictating an email, you stop to check a date, and you go quiet for half a minute. Dictation stops. Press the shortcut again and carry on. Keep the next sentence in your head before you pause, and the restart will feel less like losing your place.

Apple points people who only want to enter text to its "Dictate messages and documents" article. That is the guide to use for text-only work.

Dictation rewards a steady rhythm. Speak one full thought, then pause and read it back. If the pause runs past 30 seconds, the session ends, and you start again. That costs a few seconds on a short note. On a long memo, it becomes a habit you need to plan around.

## How Voice Control works, and what it adds

Voice Control is the bigger tool. It has four modes, and each one suits a different job.

Dictation mode types word by word, and it is on by default. Spelling mode types character by character, which helps with passwords and web addresses. Command mode responds only to commands. Swift mode writes Swift code from spoken syntax. If you do not write Swift, you can leave that mode alone.

Command mode is the one people overlook. Use it when you want the Mac to act, not type. Say "Open Mail" while Voice Control is on, and Mail opens.

Spelling mode suits short strings, such as a password, a local network address, or a long web link. Full sentences belong in Dictation mode, the default, which types word by word.

Plan for the first run. Voice Control needs the Mac to connect to the internet once, for a one-time file download from Apple. After that, Apple says you do not need an internet connection to use it. If you work on a plane or on a locked-down network, turn Voice Control on at home first.

To turn it on, choose Apple menu > System Settings > Accessibility > Voice Control, then switch it on. Scroll the sidebar if you cannot see Voice Control.

## Which one should you use?

Choose by the job, not by the feature list.

- Email, notes, and documents: standard Dictation is enough. Keep Voice Control off.
- Opening apps and moving around the Mac by voice: Voice Control, in Dictation or Command mode.
- Passwords, web addresses, and codes: Voice Control in Spelling mode.
- Swift code by voice: Voice Control in Swift mode.

Accessibility matters here. The CDC says 28.7% of U.S. adults have some type of disability. The CDC page does not name a data source for that figure, so treat it as a rough picture. For people who find a keyboard or mouse hard to use, Voice Control can be the main way they use the Mac.

I could not find a survey on how many Mac users rely on each tool. This page does not give a usage share. Be wary of any article that gives one without a source.

## Running both in one workday

Many people need both tools in one day. A writer might draft a long section by dictation, then ask the Mac to open a file or switch windows. You can do that, but not at the same time. Pick a sequence.

Here is one order that works. Start with Voice Control off and draft with standard Dictation. When you need the Mac to act, turn on Voice Control and say the command, such as "Open Notes." When the commands are done, turn Voice Control off and go back to Dictation.

Each switch takes a trip to System Settings. Batch your commands together so you make fewer trips. Finish a set of commands before you go back to drafting, and the switching cost stays low.

## What to check when Dictation seems to stop working

Start with the switch that matters most. Open System Settings > Accessibility > Voice Control. If Voice Control is on, standard Dictation is unavailable. Turn Voice Control off, then try again.

Next, check Dictation itself. Open System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation and confirm it is turned on. Look at the shortcut shown there. Press the shortcut you see on screen, not the one you remember from another Mac.

Then ask whether the silence rule ended it. Dictation stops after 30 seconds with no speech. If you paused, that may be the whole story. Press the shortcut again.

If you just turned on Voice Control for the first time, check the internet. The first turn-on needs a connection for the one-time download from Apple.

## Is dictation faster than typing?

People often quote one study on this. It is a 2016 paper by Ruan, Wobbrock, Liou, Ng, and Landay, posted on arXiv. It found English speech input 3.0x faster than a miniature smartphone keyboard. The English error rate was 20.4% lower with speech.

Those numbers have limits. The study used smartphones with built-in iOS keyboards, not Macs. The abstract also does not say whether people copied given text or wrote their own. Your writing may not match that setup.

The gap may also depend on what you write. Short replies and long drafts ask different things of your voice and your attention. The abstract I checked does not test that split, so run your own numbers.

Treat 3.0x as a hint, not a promise. Your voice, your room, and your microphone all change the result. Try the [speaking versus typing test](/tools/speaking-vs-typing-test) to see your own numbers. You can also run the [dictation accuracy checker](/tools/dictation-accuracy-checker) on your own speech.

## Edge cases that catch people out

A few cases are less obvious. Test each one once, before a deadline depends on it.

- Managed or shared Macs. If the Voice Control switch will not change, the Mac may be managed by someone else. Ask whoever runs the machine before you spend time troubleshooting.
- Different apps. Dictation types into the field you click. Some apps, such as certain web forms, may behave differently from Mail or Notes. Dictate one paragraph in each app you use most, and note what works.
- Mid-sentence switches. Changing tools while you are still speaking risks losing the thread. Finish the sentence, then switch. A clean break point costs nothing.

## Mistakes people make when they switch

Watch for these five slips.

- Expecting both to run at once. They do not. Pick one for the task in front of you.
- Looking for the old System Preferences path. On macOS 27, the path is System Settings.
- Expecting Voice Control to work offline on first use. It needs internet once.
- Losing a sentence to Dictation's 30-second stop. Press the shortcut again and keep going.
- Leaving Voice Control on before a session of plain typing. Turn it off first.

## How to switch between Voice Control and Dictation

Switching takes one trip to System Settings each way. To switch to Voice Control, choose Apple menu > System Settings > Accessibility > Voice Control and turn it on. To switch back, turn it off in the same place. If you need Dictation, open System Settings > Keyboard > Dictation and turn it on there.

Before a big writing session, run a quick check. Confirm that Voice Control is off if you want standard Dictation. Confirm your Dictation shortcut. Then dictate one test sentence into a blank note. That takes seconds, and it saves you from a stalled draft.
