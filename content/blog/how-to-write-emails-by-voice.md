To write emails by voice on a Mac, click into the message body, turn on Dictation, and speak the point of the email the way you would brief a colleague. Then scan the names, the numbers, and the one thing you are asking for before you send.

Most people skip that last step, and it is the one that matters. Dictation types what you say. A spoken email often reads like a transcript unless you speak in short, plain sentences and check the result. This guide covers the built-in options, the setup on macOS, a way to speak that avoids the transcript feel, and the errors to catch before you send.

## Why dictated emails sound like a transcript

Spoken language is loose. You might say “so, um, basically, what I meant was” before you reach the point. At a keyboard you delete that without thinking. Dictation writes it down.

Four things give a transcript away:

- Long sentences joined by commas, where you paused to breathe.
- Filler words such as “um,” “like,” and “you know.”
- No paragraph breaks, so the greeting, the ask, and the sign-off run together.
- Names and numbers that come out wrong, such as “Gordon” for Jordan or “v one point four” for v1.4.

The fix is not to speak more carefully. Speak in shorter units, then expect one editing pass. Treat the first version as a rough draft, and clean it up before anyone else sees it.

## Built-in tools that can write emails by voice

You can start without buying anything. Four built-in options cover most email work, and each one has a different limit.

| Option | Where it types | What you need | Limit to know |
|---|---|---|---|
| Apple Dictation on Mac | Text fields across Mac apps, including Mail | Dictation turned on in System Settings | Stops after 30 seconds without detected speech |
| Outlook Message > Dictate | The Outlook message body | A Microsoft 365 subscription, a microphone, and a stable internet connection | Default language is English (United States) |
| Google Docs voice typing | Google Docs only | Tools > Voice typing in Chrome, Edge, or Safari | English only, and Google’s help page does not mention Gmail |
| Gboard voice typing on iPhone | Text areas in apps that use Gboard | Gboard installed, then tap the microphone icon | Does not work with all languages |

Outlook is the most complete option, if you already pay for Microsoft 365. Microsoft says the dictation feature is only available to subscribers. In Outlook for Mac, open a new message and choose Message > Dictate, or press Option + F1. The gear icon in the Options tab lets you change the spoken language, the microphone, Auto Punctuation, and a profanity filter. That filter masks flagged words with asterisks.

Microsoft also says accuracy drops with background noise, an unstable connection, or fast speech. Plan for that before a call or a noisy café.

Gmail is the awkward case. Google’s voice typing help covers Google Docs, and it does not mention Gmail. So dictation inside Gmail is not a documented feature. In practice, many Mac users dictate into Gmail’s compose box with Apple Dictation. That is an inference, so test it on a draft before a busy morning depends on it.

On iPhone, Gboard fills the gap. Its help page lists spoken punctuation, including Period, Comma, Exclamation point, Question mark, New line, and New paragraph.

## Set up Dictation on your Mac for email

Apple’s guide for macOS 27 and later sets up dictation in four moves. Earlier macOS versions can differ, so check the path if your menus look different.

1. Choose Apple menu > System Settings, then click Keyboard.
2. Go to Dictation and turn it on. If macOS asks you to click Enable, do that.
3. Leave Auto-punctuation on for email. In supported languages, Dictation inserts commas, periods, and question marks for you.
4. Pick how you will start it: the Microphone key if your keyboard has one, the Dictation keyboard shortcut, or Edit > Start Dictation inside the app.

Apple’s guide does not name a default shortcut. Read the shortcut shown in the Dictation settings and use that one. That way you are not hunting for a key in the middle of a reply.

Check the privacy setting too. Apple says you can see in Keyboard settings whether general Dictation is processed on your device and not sent to Siri servers.

If you run macOS 27 with Apple Intelligence, an on-device model improves punctuation and capitalization. That works only in English, on M3 or later Macs with at least 12GB of unified memory. Everyone else gets the standard result, so budget more time for edits.

## How to dictate an email step by step

Say you need to answer Maya about a delivery date. Click into the body of the reply, start Dictation, and say this in one or two breaths:

> “Reply to Maya, friendly and short. We can ship Thursday if the contract is signed by Tuesday. Ask if that works. Offer a call Wednesday afternoon.”

Notice what that brief does. It names the recipient, the tone, the condition, the ask, and the next step. You are not dictating a finished email. You are dictating its parts in plain order, and you fix the rest during the scan.

Dictation has a few habits you should plan around:

- Apple stops after 30 seconds without detected speech. If you pause longer, start it again and pick up where you left off.
- Apple says there is no time limit on how long dictation runs. The real limit is attention. Split a long email into two or three short dictations, one per paragraph.
- Spoken punctuation helps when auto-punctuation misses. In Google Docs and Gboard, say “new paragraph” to start a fresh paragraph. Say “question mark” when a question needs one.

Keep a short check in your head as you speak. Every sentence should hold one thought. If you hear yourself say “and also,” start a new sentence.

## Speak the brief, not the letter

This is where most spoken emails go wrong. People try to speak the finished letter, with all the throat-clearing of a written note. Here is the same message in both forms.

What people say when they try to write a letter by voice:

> “Hi Maya, I hope you are doing well. I was thinking about the timeline, and I wanted to see if maybe Thursday could work, um, if that is okay with you.”

What to say so the email comes out clean:

> “Reply to Maya, friendly. Ship Thursday if the contract is signed by Tuesday. Ask if that works. Offer a call.”

The first version asks for permission. The second has the same content and a clear ask. Speak the second version, then type the greeting and sign-off yourself. That takes seconds.

A few rules keep the brief clean:

- Put the recipient and the ask in the first sentence.
- Keep one ask per email. Two asks mean two emails, or a short list you type yourself.
- Say dates with the day and the time, such as “Thursday at 10 a.m. Pacific.”
- Spell out hard names when the tool hesitates. “Maya, M-A-Y-A” is quicker than fixing it later.
- Name your tone in one word, such as friendly, firm, apologetic, or brief.

## Scan the draft before you send

Dictation mistakes are predictable. Check for them in the same order every time, and the scan takes less time than you expect.

| Check | What dictation often does | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Names and product terms | Mishears “Jordan” as “Gordon” | Type names and product terms by hand |
| Numbers and versions | Writes “v one point four” | Type versions, dates, and amounts yourself |
| Sentence breaks | Runs two thoughts into one sentence | Split at each full stop |
| Greeting and sign-off | Merges “Hi Maya I hope” into one line | Add a line break after the greeting |
| Filler | Keeps “um” and “basically” | Delete them |
| The ask | Buries it in the middle | Move the ask up, near the top |

Then read the email once out loud. Your ear catches run-on sentences faster than your eyes do. If a sentence makes you run out of breath, it is too long for email. Split it.

Last, check the To field. A spoken name can match the wrong contact, and a wrong recipient is the most expensive mistake a dictated email can make.

## How accurate is voice email writing?

The best evidence I found is a 2016 study of short messages. Researchers from Stanford and Baidu compared speech with keyboard entry on an iPhone 6 Plus, in English and Mandarin. The task was short messages, not long business email, so treat the numbers as a guide, not a promise.

| Measure, English short messages | Speech | Keyboard |
|---|---|---|
| Entry speed | 153 WPM | 52 WPM |
| Corrected error rate during entry | 5.30% | 11.22% |
| Uncorrected error rate in the final text | 1.30% | 0.79% |

Speech was 2.93 times faster in English. The corrected error rate was lower for speech, but the uncorrected error rate in the final text was slightly higher. That gap is small. It still means the scan step earns its minute, because one missed name is the error a reader remembers.

So voice is quicker to get words down, and you still need to read what you send. Faster drafts are only useful if the final text is right.

## Why email takes so long to write

A figure you will see often says knowledge workers spend 28% of their workweek managing email. It traces to a 2012 McKinsey report. The version that circulates comes from a secondary blog, so treat the number as dated and rough.

Voice does not fix the inbox. It shortens one part of the job, getting the body of a reply down when typing is slow. Triage, search, and deciding what to answer still take your attention. ## Common mistakes when you write emails by voice on Mac

1. Dictating a full letter word for word. Speak the brief, then write the sentences you care about most.
2. Skipping the scan. The final-text error rate is small, but names and numbers are where a small rate hurts.
3. Dictating in a scratch app and pasting later. That adds a step and another place for text to change. Dictate into the compose window you will send from.
4. Trusting names. Check the greeting and the To field every time.
5. Assuming every app supports dictation. Google’s voice typing is English only and documented for Docs. Test Gmail and Outlook once before you rely on them.
6. Forgetting the Microsoft 365 requirement. Outlook’s Dictate is only for subscribers, so check your plan first.
7. Dictating in a noisy room. Microsoft says background noise lowers accuracy.

Speaking slowly is not the fix. A steady pace and short sentences are.
