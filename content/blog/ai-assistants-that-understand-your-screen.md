An AI assistant that understands your screen reads the app, window, selection, or document you have open, then uses that context to answer your request. You do not need to copy the text, take a screenshot, or explain what "this" or "that" refers to, because the assistant can already see it.

That is the simple version. The details decide whether the tool helps you or makes you nervous. This guide explains what these assistants do in 2026, how Microsoft, Apple, and Google handle screen access, what to check before you turn one on, and where a Mac voice assistant fits in.

## What "understands your screen" actually means

Three different abilities sit under that label. They are easy to mix up, so keep them apart when you compare products.

The first is reading. The assistant gets the text in the window. It knows the email you are answering or the paragraph you have highlighted.

The second is looking. The assistant gets a picture of the screen. This helps with charts, images, and layouts that have no clean text behind them.

The third is acting. The assistant changes something in the app for you, such as filling in a field or sending a message. This is the rarest of the three. Most products stop at explaining.

The permission a product asks for tells you a lot. On a Mac, screen-aware tools usually ask for Screen Recording, and some also ask for Accessibility. The guide [Accessibility Permission Mac AI: The Two Permissions Your AI Assistant Asks For](/blog/accessibility-vs-screen-recording-ai-permissions) explains what each one allows.

Ask the vendor one direct question: do you read text, look at pictures, or both? A vague answer is a warning sign.

## What you can ask a screen-aware assistant to do

The request gets shorter when the context is already on screen. You stop writing background paragraphs. You say what you want done with what is there.

The table below shows common cases. The last column matters most, because it tells you what to check before you use the output.

| Where you are | Short request | Useful result | Check before you use it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gmail thread | "Yes to Thursday, keep it warm" | A reply that uses the names and dates in the thread | Did it pick the right Thursday? |
| Slack conversation | "Summarize this and list my next steps" | A short summary with owners and deadlines | Did it miss messages above the visible area? |
| Cursor file | "Add error handling and explain the change" | An instruction you can paste into an agent | Did it touch code you did not mention? |
| Notion page | "Turn this into a project brief" | Headings, a goal, and open questions | Did it invent a deadline that is not on the page? |
| Document paragraph | "Make this clearer for a non-expert" | A rewrite that keeps your facts | Did a name or a number change? |

### A worked example: a reply in a long thread

Say a colleague asks in a long email thread whether you can move a review to Thursday. The thread has many replies, and the important date sits near the top. A screen-aware assistant reads the visible messages. You say, "Yes to Thursday, ask them to send the deck first."

A good draft uses the right day and asks for the deck. It does not promise a time you have not confirmed. Before you send, you still check your calendar and the tone. The assistant saves the typing. You keep the decision.

### A worked example: a code file

Open a file in your editor and ask for a change. You say, "Add error handling to the save function and explain the change briefly." The request is short, but it still makes sense because the function is on screen. The assistant can see the function name, the code around it, and the calls that depend on it.

The output you want is a clear change plus a reason you can review. A common mistake is asking for "a fix" with no file open. The assistant then guesses which file you mean, and the guess is often wrong.

## Where the big platforms stand in 2026

Each large platform has taken a different route. The details matter more than the marketing, so read each product's own support page before you trust a claim.

### Microsoft Copilot Vision on Windows

Microsoft's support page says you can share your desktop screen or phone camera and ask Copilot about what is shown. Vision works only during a voice conversation with Copilot.

Microsoft also says Copilot Vision can highlight parts of the screen, but it does not act for you. It won't click, type, or scroll. Personal accounts need a Microsoft 365 Personal, Family, or Premium subscription. Work and school accounts need a Microsoft Copilot license. You also need microphone access.

To stop sharing, select Stop sharing. That ends the screen share and keeps the voice chat open. Each vision conversation is separate, so Copilot does not reuse earlier screens. Microsoft says availability depends on region and language, and that features are still rolling out.

### Siri AI on Mac

Apple describes onscreen awareness in Siri AI as the ability to "ask about and act on what's on your screen." Siri AI is in beta. Apple also lists several limits that matter before you turn it on.

- Languages: English, French, Japanese, Korean, Portuguese, and Spanish. Your device language and your Siri language must match.
- Regions: not available with a mainland China region. In the EU, it is available on macOS and visionOS for EU Apple Account regions, but not on iOS, iPadOS, or watchOS.
- Age: not available to users under 13.
- Usage: server-side features have daily usage limits that may vary.

To turn it on, go to Settings > Siri and select "Try Siri AI (Beta)." Apple's page does not describe a dedicated setting to turn off onscreen awareness. Read that twice before you enable it. For the keyboard side of the feature, see [Siri screen Mac Shift Command 6: What It Does With Your Screen on macOS 27](/blog/siri-ai-mac-screen-shortcut).

Do not treat older promises as the current list. Check Apple's page for which apps and actions work today.

### Gemini screen actions on Android

Google's Gemini Help says screen actions appear when you open the Gemini app over an app, video, or file. They offer shortcuts that use screenshots, PDFs, and URLs from your screen. The "Ask about" chip lets you ask about the screen, a file, or a webpage.

Watch out for auto-submit. Every screen action except "Ask about" sends screen content as soon as you tap it. To stop that, press and hold a suggestion chip and tap "Turn off auto-submit."

Gemini's Intelligent Screen Context lets Gemini decide when to use what is visible. It works only when you use Gemini in English.

To turn screen context off on Android, go to profile picture > Settings > Screen context. Then turn off "Use text from screen" and "Use screenshot."

This is a phone feature, so it does not help with your Mac directly. It still shows the pattern: a visible shortcut, a chance to ask about the screen, and a setting you can switch off.

## How to check what an assistant can see and keep

Products describe their screen access in different places. Check these five points before you give access to a window with client names, pay details, or medical notes.

1. When is the screen read? Only during a request, or in the background?
2. Is it text, a picture, or both?
3. Where does the content go? Some work happens on the device, and some goes to a cloud model.
4. Is anything kept, and for how long? Look for the product's privacy or retention page.
5. How do you turn it off? If the only answer is uninstalling the app, read the policy closely before you install it.

For Copilot Vision, Stop sharing ends the screen share. For Gemini on Android, the Screen context settings let you switch off text and screenshot use. For Siri AI, Apple's page does not describe a dedicated off switch, so check the Settings path and Apple's privacy material before you enable it.

## What the numbers say about AI and on-screen tasks

No survey in the research measures screen-reading assistants directly. The closest numbers track general AI use and tasks that involve reading a screen.

- WebAIM's Screen Reader User Survey #11 ran from July to August 2026 and had 1,780 valid responses. The sample was not controlled, so treat the results as a direction rather than a precise share. Of respondents, 40.6% use AI for guidance in performing an online task, 37.1% use it for summaries of webpages or documents, and 60.1% use it to generate image descriptions or alt text.
- Pew Research surveyed 5,119 US adults from February 17 to 23, 2026. Of them, 49% say they ever use AI chatbots, up from 33% in 2024.
- Menlo Ventures' 2026 consumer AI report, based on a July 2026 survey of 5,067 US adults, found that 41% of AI users have tried an AI agent. Of those users, 32% have let AI act on their behalf at least once without final approval.

Read the Menlo figure as a reason to set your own rule. Mine is simple. The assistant drafts, and I send. Letting an AI act without a final check is common, so decide in advance where that check happens for you.

## Mistakes people make with screen-aware assistants

- Assuming it knows which window you mean. If two threads are open, name the one you want, such as "the Acme thread, not the newsletter."
- Sending the output without reading it. Gemini's auto-submit shows how easy it is to fire off a request by accident.
- Leaving sensitive windows open. Close your password manager and bank statements before you ask about the page next to them.
- Expecting clicks. Copilot Vision won't click, type, or scroll, so you still do the clicking yourself.
- Mismatching languages. Siri AI needs your device language and Siri language to match, and Gemini's Intelligent Screen Context needs English.
- Forgetting the permission after a test. When you stop using a tool, revoke its screen access in the same system settings where you granted it.

## Start with one low-risk test

Pick one tool and one harmless window, such as a public article or a test document. Ask for one short task, such as a summary of the visible text. Read the answer, find out where the content went, and locate the off switch.

Only after that test should you open a real client thread. If the tool passes, give it a small role. If it fails, you learned that without risking anything that matters.
