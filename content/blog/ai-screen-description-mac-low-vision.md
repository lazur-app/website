A Mac can describe images with AI today, but only where VoiceOver can read them, such as web pages. Turn on image descriptions in VoiceOver Utility, press VO-Shift-L on a page, and VoiceOver uses on-device intelligence to describe the images it finds.

Describing your whole screen on command is a narrower story. Apple has not listed Mac steps for Image Explorer, and the camera feature it documents is an iPhone feature. This guide covers what Apple ships, how to turn it on, where it falls short, and what to check before you trust a description.

## What Apple offers on Mac right now

Apple does not have one button that describes everything on screen. It has several features with different scopes. Some are documented for Mac. Some are documented for iPhone. A few were announced without a platform attached.

Start with Apple's Accessibility Features page. It says "Image Descriptions and Image Explorer are now available for macOS." It also says Image Explorer "uses Apple Intelligence to give richer descriptions of images on your screen." That is the clearest Mac statement Apple makes.

The same page lists availability for VoiceOver Live Recognition and Image Explorer under iOS and iPadOS. So the page reads two ways. Image descriptions are clearly on the Mac list. Image Explorer is less clear, and I could not find Mac steps for it.

Apple's May 19, 2026 newsroom release called these upgrades "coming later this year" and gave no month. The release also says VoiceOver and Magnifier "should not be relied upon in circumstances where one could be harmed or injured." Apple Intelligence is still labeled beta. Some features are not offered in every region or language.

macOS 27 Golden Gate shipped September 14, 2026, according to Wikipedia, which cites MacRumors. Point releases 27.0.1 (September 28, 2026) and 27.2 (October 26, 2026) followed. Check your version under Apple menu > About This Mac before you test anything.

| Feature | What Apple says | Mac status |
|---|---|---|
| VoiceOver image descriptions | Describes images on web pages and apps with on-device intelligence | Documented in Apple's Mac VoiceOver guide |
| Image Explorer | Richer descriptions of images on screen, including scanned bills and personal records | Listed for macOS on the features page, no Mac steps found |
| Live Recognition | Asks about the camera view after you press the Action button | Documented for iPhone, Mac not confirmed |
| Vision features on macOS 27 | Speak onscreen text, Zoom, Magnifier, Hover Text, Audio Descriptions, Accessibility Reader | Page does not mention image descriptions, Image Explorer or Live Recognition |

## How to turn on AI image descriptions in VoiceOver on Mac

This is the part you can set up today. The steps follow Apple's guide for navigating web pages by images on Mac. The guide's download file name suggests it was written for macOS Tahoe 26, so a menu name may differ in macOS 27. If it does, search for "image" inside VoiceOver Utility.

1. Turn on VoiceOver. Open System Settings > Accessibility > VoiceOver and switch it on.
2. Open VoiceOver Utility. Press VO-F8. By default, VO is Control and Option, so the shortcut is ⌃⌥F8.
3. Go to the VoiceOver Recognition category and turn on image descriptions.
4. Open a web page with photos, such as a product listing or a news story.
5. Press VO-Shift-L (⌃⌥⇧L). VoiceOver uses on-device intelligence to describe the images on the page.
6. Move between images with VO-Command-G (⌃⌥⌘G). Go back with VO-Command-Shift-G (⌃⌥⌘⇧G).
7. If an image has no description, VoiceOver speaks its filename. To fix that, go to VoiceOver Utility > Web > Navigate Images and create a custom label.

Here is a common case. You are shopping for a lamp on a site with thin alt text. You press VO-Shift-L, and VoiceOver describes the lamp photo. If the description says only "lamp," you move to the next image. Then you read the product title and specs with normal VoiceOver reading. Text on the page is more reliable than a guess about a photo.

## Why a screen reader and an image describer do different jobs

A screen holds text, buttons, menus and pictures. VoiceOver already reads text and controls well. Image descriptions fill the gap where a picture has no label. A chart, a scanned receipt or a photo pasted into an email can be a picture with no words for the reader to find.

AFB's 2026 report shows how people use these tools. Reading text was the most common use among blind and low vision respondents, at 93%. Describing photos and describing scenes were each reported by 78%. So text inside images matters as much as the pictures themselves.

Speak onscreen text covers much of the text side on Mac. Apple's Mac vision page for macOS 27 lists it, along with Zoom, Magnifier, Hover Text and Audio Descriptions. The same page does not mention VoiceOver image descriptions, Image Explorer or Live Recognition. Use the vision features for what they cover, and treat AI descriptions as an extra layer.

Consider a scanned bill saved as a photo. It is an image, not selectable text. Apple says Image Explorer covers scanned bills and personal records. Because I could not confirm Mac steps for it, test it on a harmless scan before you rely on it for a real bill.

## How accurate are AI descriptions for low vision?

Accuracy is the question people ask most, and the honest answer is mixed. The best public data comes from AFB's Innovation for Access report. It is based on a 2025 survey and was published in August 2026.

In that survey, 51% of blind and low vision respondents used AI for visual descriptions at least once a day. Only 8% of all visual description users called the results "extremely accurate." Another 57% said "mostly accurate." AFB does not break those two numbers out for blind and low vision users alone.

The harm numbers matter more. AFB reports that 61 blind and low vision users, or 21% of that group, had an AI description error that caused harm. The comparison group was 14 sighted disabled users, or 9%. AFB links the gap to a simple problem. People who cannot see the image cannot check the output.

Those figures do not mean descriptions are useless. They mean a wrong answer costs more when you cannot look at the picture to confirm it. Use descriptions as a first pass. Confirm anything that involves money, medicine, a signature, a deadline or a safety warning with a second source.

Scale explains why this matters. The 2024 National Health Interview Survey found 49.5 million US adults aged 18 and over reported some difficulty seeing. The same survey found 3.8 million had a lot of trouble seeing even with glasses, and 420,000 could not see at all. WHO says at least 2.2 billion people worldwide have distance or near vision impairment.

## Run a ten-minute test before you rely on it

Reading about accuracy is one thing. Testing your own setup is better. Pick three pages you visit every week. Good choices are a news story with a photo, an online store and a recipe site with step photos.

Run the same test on each page. Press VO-Shift-L and let VoiceOver finish. Note how many images it described and how many it read by filename. Then move through the images with VO-Command-G. For each one, ask a plain question. Did the description match the picture? Did it leave out something you needed?

Write the results in a plain note with three columns: page, what was right and what was wrong. After three pages you will know which kinds of images you can trust. A store photo of a jacket may hold up well. A chart may not.

If a wrong description could cost you something, mark it in the note too. Those are the cases to confirm with a person, a magnifier or the printed label. Repeat the test after each macOS update, because Apple still labels parts of this feature set as beta.

## Mistakes that make AI descriptions less useful

Most problems come from a few habits. Fix these first.

Expecting a description on every image. Some images have no description, and VoiceOver reads the filename instead. A filename like IMG_4471.jpg tells you nothing. Add a custom label for images you visit often.

Testing on a page you have never seen. Pick a page you know well. Then you can tell whether the description matches what you expect.

Skipping the region and language check. Apple says Apple Intelligence is in beta, and some features are not in every region or language. If a feature is missing, check your region and language before you blame the Mac.

Assuming it works the same in every app. Apple's features page says VoiceOver Recognition covers web pages and apps, including ones without accessibility information. The steps above cover web pages. Test apps one at a time.

Trusting one answer for something that matters. Apple's own footnote says VoiceOver should not be relied upon where someone could be harmed. Treat that line as the rule.

## Privacy: where the screenshots go

Apple's Mac guide says VoiceOver uses on-device intelligence to describe web images. That is the Apple path. Third-party assistants work differently. Many send a screenshot to a cloud model to answer you, and the rules differ from app to app.

Before you give any assistant Screen Recording or Accessibility access, read its privacy policy and find out where screenshots go.
